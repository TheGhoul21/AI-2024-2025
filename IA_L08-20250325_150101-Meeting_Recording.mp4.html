Introduction

This lecture delves into the use of propositional logic as a foundational tool for building more intelligent agents. We explore how this formal language allows agents to represent knowledge explicitly and perform logical reasoning, moving beyond the capabilities of simpler reflex or goal-based agents discussed previously.

Recap: Knowledge-Based Agents

We previously introduced the paradigm of knowledge-based agents. These agents maintain an internal Knowledge Base (KB), which is a collection of sentences expressed in a formal language, representing what the agent knows about the world. The core components and cycle of operation involve:

  • A Knowledge Base (KB): Stores facts and rules about the environment. Initially, it contains background knowledge; later, it incorporates information derived from percepts.

  • An Inference Mechanism (Engine): A computational process that derives new sentences (knowledge) logically implied by the sentences already in the KB.

  • A Tell operation: Used to add new sentences to the KB, typically derived from the agent’s perceptions of the environment.

  • An Ask operation: Used to query the KB. The agent asks what actions are appropriate or what the properties of the world are, based on its current knowledge and inferred conclusions.

The agent’s intelligence and ability to act rationally depend crucially on the richness of its KB and the capabilities of its inference mechanism. Propositional logic provides a concrete framework for implementing both the representation (KB) and the reasoning (Ask/Tell involving inference).

The Wumpus World Revisited

We continue to use the Wumpus World as a canonical example environment for knowledge-based agents. It serves as an effective testbed because success requires non-trivial reasoning about hidden aspects of the environment.

Agent’s Goal and Performance Measure:

The agent starts at square [1,1], facing East. The goal is to navigate the grid, find the gold, return to [1,1], and execute the ‘Climb’ action to exit. The performance measure typically involves:

  • +1000 points for climbing out with the gold.

  • -1000 points for falling into a pit or being eaten by the Wumpus (death).

  • -10 points for using the arrow.

  • -1 point for each action taken (encouraging efficiency).

Task Environment Characteristics (PEAS Analysis)

Analyzing the Wumpus World according to the standard dimensions helps clarify the challenges the agent faces:

  • Observability: Partially Observable. The agent’s sensors provide only local information: stench (adjacent Wumpus), breeze (adjacent pit), glitter (gold in current square), bump (hit a wall), scream (Wumpus killed). Crucial information is not directly sensed:

    • The agent’s absolute location must be tracked internally based on movement actions.

    • Whether the Wumpus is alive or dead must be inferred from hearing (or not hearing) the scream after shooting.

    • Whether the agent possesses the arrow must be tracked internally after a ‘Shoot’ action.

    This partial observability necessitates maintaining an internal state representing beliefs about the unobserved aspects of the world.

  • Agents: Single Agent. Although the Wumpus and pits pose threats, they are static elements of the environment. They do not act independently or strategically. The only active entity changing the state of the world through its actions is the agent itself.

  • Determinism: Deterministic. The outcome of every action is uniquely determined by the state in which it is performed. Moving forward into square [x,y] always results in being in [x,y] if it’s empty, hitting a wall if it’s a boundary, or dying if it contains a pit or the Wumpus (unless killed).

  • Episodic/Sequential: Sequential. The agent’s task involves a sequence of actions. The choice of action at each step depends on past actions and percepts, and significantly impacts future possibilities and the eventual outcome. A single wrong move can lead to failure.

  • Static/Dynamic: Static. The locations of pits, the Wumpus (while alive), and the gold do not change while the agent is deliberating or performing an action. The environment only changes in response to the agent’s actions (e.g., Wumpus becoming dead, agent changing location).

  • Discrete/Continuous: Discrete. The environment is a grid of discrete locations (4x4). Time proceeds in discrete steps (turns). The agent’s percepts and actions are also discrete.

  • Known/Unknown: The rules of the environment (the "physics" – how breezes relate to pits, stenches to the Wumpus, effects of actions) are known to the agent. However, the initial configuration of the environment – specifically, the locations of the pits and the Wumpus (and the gold) – is unknown.

    • From one viewpoint, the transition model is fully known (e.g., the agent knows that entering a pit square leads to death), but the current state description is incomplete.

    • Alternatively, one could argue the transition model is effectively unknown initially, as the agent doesn’t know the outcome of moving into an adjacent square (safe or death). This distinction is subtle and doesn’t significantly alter the approach needed. The key point is the presence of critical unknown information.

Need for Reasoning beyond Simple Exploration

The partial observability and the potentially lethal nature of the Wumpus World make simple exploration strategies (like random movement or uninformed search on the grid locations) extremely risky. An agent that blindly enters an adjacent square might fall into a pit or encounter the Wumpus.

To survive and succeed, the agent must use logical reasoning. It needs to:

  1. Combine its knowledge of the Wumpus World rules with the percepts received.

  2. Infer properties of unobserved squares, such as whether they are safe or likely contain a hazard.

  3. Make decisions (which square to explore next, whether to shoot) based on these inferences.

This motivates the use of formal logic as the mechanism for representing knowledge and performing inference within the agent.

Logic Fundamentals

To build agents capable of reasoning, we need a formal system for representing knowledge and deriving conclusions. Logic provides the necessary mathematical foundation.

Formal Languages: Representation and Derivation

Logics are formal languages designed for knowledge representation and reasoning. For our purposes, a logic provides two key capabilities:

  1. Representation: A precise syntax defines how to construct sentences (formulas) that represent facts, rules, and beliefs about the world. These sentences form the agent’s Knowledge Base (KB).

  2. Derivation (Inference): A set of rules or procedures allows the agent to systematically derive new sentences (conclusions) that are logical consequences of the sentences already present in the KB. This process allows the agent to make explicit knowledge that was only implicit in its KB.

Example : Wumpus World Inference If an agent’s KB contains the rule "A square is breezy if and only if an adjacent square contains a pit" and the fact "Square [1,1] is not breezy", the agent can use logical inference to derive the conclusions "Square [1,2] does not contain a pit" and "Square [2,1] does not contain a pit". Similarly, if the KB contains "Square [1,1] has no stench", the agent can infer "Square [1,2] does not contain the Wumpus" and "Square [2,1] does not contain the Wumpus".

Syntax and Semantics

Every logic is characterized by its syntax and semantics, which are distinct but related concepts.

Syntax: The Structure of Sentences

The syntax of a logic defines the rules for constructing valid sentences, often called Well-Formed Formulas (WFFs). It specifies:

  • The set of allowed symbols (e.g., propositional symbols like \(P, Q\), connectives like \(\land, \lor, \neg\), quantifiers like \(\forall, \exists\)).

  • Grammatical rules for combining these symbols into complex sentences.

Syntax determines whether a string of symbols constitutes a legitimate sentence in the language, irrespective of its meaning. For example, in propositional logic, \(P \land \neg Q\) might be syntactically correct, while \(P \neg \land Q\) would be incorrect.

Semantics: The Meaning and Truth of Sentences

The semantics of a logic defines the meaning of sentences by connecting them to possible states of the world. It provides a way to determine the truth value (True or False) of any sentence.

  • Models (Possible Worlds): Semantics is often defined in terms of models. A model is a formal abstraction of a possible state of affairs or a "possible world". For instance, in the Wumpus World, each possible configuration of pits and Wumpus location corresponds to a different model.

  • Interpretation: Semantics specifies how to interpret the symbols of the language within a given model.

  • Truth Evaluation: Based on the interpretation, semantics defines rules for evaluating the truth value of any sentence within a specific model. A sentence \(\alpha\) might be true in model \(m_1\) but false in model \(m_2\).

Example : Semantics in Wumpus World Let \(W_{\{,1},3\}\) be a sentence meaning "The Wumpus is in square [1,3]".

  • In a model \(m_1\) where the Wumpus is actually at [1,3], the sentence \(\Wumpus{1,3}\) is True.

  • In a model \(m_2\) where the Wumpus is at [2,2], the sentence \(\Wumpus{1,3}\) is False.

  • If the agent shoots into [1,3] in model \(m_1\) and kills the Wumpus, this transitions the world to a new model \(m_3\). In \(m_3\), the sentence \(WumpusAlive^{}\) (meaning "The Wumpus is alive") would become False, even though it was True in \(m_1\).

Think of natural language.

  • Syntax is like grammar. "Colorless green ideas sleep furiously" is syntactically correct English, even if nonsensical. "Sleep ideas colorless furiously green" is syntactically incorrect.

  • Semantics deals with meaning and truth. "The Eiffel Tower is in Paris" is syntactically correct and semantically true (in the real world model). "The Eiffel Tower is in Rome" is syntactically correct but semantically false.

Syntax determines if a sentence is well-formed; semantics determines if it is true in a given context (model).

Entailment (Logical Consequence)

A central concept in logic is entailment, which captures the idea of one sentence necessarily following from others.

Definition : Entailment (\(\models\)) A set of sentences \(\textit{KB}{}\) entails a sentence \(\beta\) (written \(\textit{KB}{} \models \beta\)) if and only if \(\beta\) is true in every model in which all sentences in \(\textit{KB}{}\) are true. Equivalently, if \(\alpha\) is a single sentence, \(\alpha \models \beta\) if \(\beta\) is true in every model where \(\alpha\) is true. \[\textit{KB}{} \models \beta \iff \text{Models}(\textit{KB}{}) \subseteq \text{Models}(\beta)\] where \(\text{Models}(\gamma)\) denotes the set of all models in which the sentence(s) \(\gamma\) are true.

If \(\textit{KB}{} \models \beta\), we say \(\beta\) is a logical consequence of \(\textit{KB}{}\). Entailment is about the relationship between sentences based purely on their meaning (semantics), regardless of any specific inference procedure.

Entailment Example in Wumpus World (via Model Checking)

Let’s revisit the scenario where the agent has visited [1,1] (finding it safe: \(\neg P_{1,1,,} \neg \Wumpus{1,1}\)) and moved to [2,1], where it perceives a breeze.

  • Knowledge Base (KB) includes:

    • \(\neg \Pit{1,1}\) (From initial state or prior visit)

    • \(\BreezeProp{2,1}\) (Inferred from percept at [2,1])

    • \(B_{2,1,\iff }(P_{1,1,\lor }P_{2,2,\lor }P_{3,1,)}\) (Rule for breeze at [2,1])

    • \(\neg \BreezeProp{1,1}\) (Inferred from percept at [1,1])

    • \(B_{1,1,\iff }(P_{1,2,\lor }P_{2,1,)}\) (Rule for breeze at [1,1])

  • Relevant Inferences from KB:

    • From \(\neg \BreezeProp{1,1}\) and its rule, we infer \(\neg (P_{1,2,\lor }P_{2,1,)}\), which implies \(\neg P_{1,2,\land }\neg \Pit{2,1}\). So, \(\neg \Pit{1,2}\) and \(\neg \Pit{2,1}\) are known.

    • From \(\BreezeProp{2,1}\) and its rule, we infer \(P_{1,1,\lor }P_{2,2,\lor }\Pit{3,1}\).

    • Combining this with \(\neg \Pit{1,1}\), we conclude \(P_{2,2,\lor }\Pit{3,1}\). There must be a pit in [2,2] or [3,1] (or both).

  • Possible Models (Focusing on Pits in [2,2], [3,1]): Considering only the locations relevant to the breeze in [2,1] and consistent with \(\textit{KB}{}\), the possible pit configurations are:

    1. \(m_1\): Pit in [2,2] only (\(P_{2,2,\land }\neg \Pit{3,1}\))

    2. \(m_2\): Pit in [3,1] only (\(\neg P_{2,2,\land }\Pit{3,1}\))

    3. \(m_3\): Pits in both [2,2] and [3,1] (\(P_{2,2,\land }\Pit{3,1}\))

    So, \(\text{Models}(\textit{KB}{}) = \{m_1, m_2, m_3\}\) (restricted to these propositions).

Now, let’s check entailment for specific queries using these models:

  • Query 1: Is \(\neg \Pit{1,2}\) entailed? (\(\textit{KB}{} \models \neg \Pit{1,2}\)?) We already inferred \(\neg \Pit{1,2}\) directly from the absence of breeze in [1,1]. Let’s verify using models. The sentence \(\neg \Pit{1,2}\) is true in all models where there is no pit in [1,2]. Since our simplified models \(m_1, m_2, m_3\) don’t constrain \(\Pit{1,2}\), we need to consider the full KB. As shown in the inference above, \(\neg \Pit{1,2}\) is derivable, meaning it must hold in all models of the KB. Thus, \(\textit{KB}{} \models \neg \Pit{1,2}\). The agent can safely conclude [1,2] has no pit.

  • Query 2: Is \(\neg \Pit{2,2}\) entailed? (\(\textit{KB}{} \models \neg \Pit{2,2}\)?) We examine the truth of \(\neg \Pit{2,2}\) in the models consistent with \(\textit{KB}{}\):

    • In \(m_1\): \(\Pit{2,2}\) is true, so \(\neg \Pit{2,2}\) is False.

    • In \(m_2\): \(\neg \Pit{2,2}\) is true.

    • In \(m_3\): \(\Pit{2,2}\) is true, so \(\neg \Pit{2,2}\) is False.

    Since \(\neg \Pit{2,2}\) is not true in all models of \(\textit{KB}{}\) (it’s false in \(m_1\) and \(m_3\)), the entailment does not hold: \(\textit{KB}{} \not\models \neg \Pit{2,2}\). The agent cannot conclude that [2,2] is safe.

  • Query 3: Is \(P_{2,2,\lor }\Pit{3,1}\) entailed? (\(\textit{KB}{} \models P_{2,2,\lor }\Pit{3,1}\)?) We check the truth of \(P_{2,2,\lor }\Pit{3,1}\) in the models:

    • In \(m_1\): \(\Pit{2,2}\) is true, so the disjunction is True.

    • In \(m_2\): \(\Pit{3,1}\) is true, so the disjunction is True.

    • In \(m_3\): Both \(\Pit{2,2}\) and \(\Pit{3,1}\) are true, so the disjunction is True.

    Since \(P_{2,2,\lor }\Pit{3,1}\) is true in all models of \(\textit{KB}{}\), the entailment holds: \(\textit{KB}{} \models P_{2,2,\lor }\Pit{3,1}\).

This process of determining entailment by enumerating all relevant models and checking the truth of the query sentence in each is called model checking.

Logical Inference: Finding Entailed Sentences

While entailment defines what logically follows, model checking provides a direct way to compute it. However, it’s not always practical. We need effective procedures, called inference algorithms, to find or derive entailed sentences.

Model Checking Approach (Semantic Inference)

This approach directly implements the definition of entailment by exploring the space of possible models.

  • Process: Enumerate models consistent with the KB. Check if the query sentence \(\beta\) holds in all of them.

  • Pros: Conceptually straightforward. Can be efficient for some types of problems, especially in propositional logic where the number of models is finite (though potentially large, \(2^n\) for \(n\) symbols). Forms the basis of algorithms like DPLL for SAT solving.

  • Cons: Becomes computationally intractable as the number of propositional symbols grows. Infeasible for logics with infinite models (like first-order logic with numbers).

Theorem Proving Approach (Syntactic Inference)

This approach uses inference rules to manipulate sentences syntactically, deriving new sentences from existing ones without explicitly constructing models.

  • Inference Rules: Syntactic patterns that license the derivation of a conclusion sentence from premise sentences. Examples include Modus Ponens, And Elimination, and Resolution (discussed later).

  • Derivation/Proof: A sequence of sentences, starting with the premises in the KB, where each subsequent sentence is derived from preceding sentences using an inference rule.

  • Notation: \(\textit{KB}{} \vdash_i \beta\) means sentence \(\beta\) can be derived (proved) from \(\textit{KB}{}\) using a specific set of inference rules \(i\).

  • Pros: Can be more efficient than model checking, especially when the proof is short or the structure of the sentences guides the search. Applicable even when the number of models is infinite. Forms the basis of automated theorem provers and logic programming.

  • Cons: Finding a proof can be computationally hard. The search space of possible derivations can be very large. Requires careful design of inference rules and search strategies.

Soundness and Completeness of Inference Algorithms

An inference procedure \(\vdash\) should ideally relate correctly to the semantic notion of entailment \(\models\). Two key properties define this relationship:

Definition : Soundness An inference algorithm \(\vdash\) is sound if it only derives sentences that are actually entailed. \[\text{If } \textit{KB}{} \vdash \beta, \text{ then } \textit{KB}{} \models \beta\] A sound algorithm never asserts a conclusion that is not a logical consequence of the premises. It preserves truth. Soundness ensures that the inference process does not invent conclusions that are not justified by the knowledge base.

Definition : Completeness An inference algorithm \(\vdash\) is complete if it can derive every sentence that is entailed. \[\text{If } \textit{KB}{} \models \beta, \text{ then } \textit{KB}{} \vdash \beta\] A complete algorithm guarantees that if a conclusion logically follows from the premises, the algorithm is capable of finding a derivation for it (given sufficient resources). Completeness ensures that the inference process is powerful enough to find all logical consequences.

Goal: We seek inference algorithms that are both sound (don’t derive falsehoods) and complete (can derive all truths). Soundness is typically essential, while completeness is highly desirable.

Overview of Logic Types

Not all logics are created equal; they differ in their expressive power and computational complexity.

Propositional Logic (PL)

  • Basic Unit: Propositions (atomic sentences) representing facts that are either true or false (e.g., \(P\), \(Q\), \(Raining\), \(\Pit{1,2}\)).

  • Syntax: Combines atomic propositions using logical connectives (\(\neg, \land, \lor, \implies, \iff\)).

  • Semantics: Defined by truth assignments to atomic propositions and truth tables for connectives.

  • Expressiveness: Limited. Treats propositions as indivisible units. Cannot easily express properties of objects, relationships between them, or make generalized statements (e.g., "All squares adjacent to a pit are breezy").

  • Complexity: Deciding entailment is decidable but co-NP-complete in general (though often feasible in practice for many problems).

First-Order Logic (FOL) (Predicate Calculus)

  • Basic Units: Objects, properties of objects (predicates), relations between objects, functions on objects.

  • Syntax: Includes terms (representing objects, e.g., constants, variables, function applications), atomic sentences (predicates applied to terms, e.g., \(Brother(Richard, John)\), \(At(Agent, [1,1])\)), connectives (like PL), and quantifiers (\(\forall\) - for all, \(\exists\) - there exists) to generalize over objects.

  • Semantics: More complex. Requires defining a domain of objects and interpretations (mappings) for constants, predicates, and functions within a model.

  • Expressiveness: Significantly greater than PL. Allows representing complex relationships and making universal or existential statements about potentially infinite domains (e.g., \(\forall x, y \: Adjacent(x, y) \land Pit(x) \implies Breeze(y)\)).

  • Complexity: Deciding entailment is undecidable in general (no algorithm exists that can always determine entailment in finite time), but it is semi-decidable (algorithms exist that can find a proof if one exists, but may run forever if no proof exists).

Note : This course will first focus on Propositional Logic due to its simpler structure, allowing us to understand the core concepts of logical representation and inference. We will explore its capabilities and limitations (like the Frame Problem) before moving on to the more expressive First-Order Logic in subsequent lectures.

Propositional Logic: Syntax and Semantics

We now formally define Propositional Logic (PL), the simplest logic we will study, focusing on its structure (syntax) and meaning (semantics).

Syntax

The syntax defines what constitutes a valid sentence in PL.

Atomic Sentences (Propositional Symbols)

The fundamental building blocks are atomic sentences or propositional symbols.

  • Each symbol represents a statement that can be definitively True or False.

  • They are indivisible units within the logic.

  • Symbols can be represented by uppercase letters (e.g., \(P, Q, R\)) or more descriptive names, often using subscripts for clarity (e.g., \(\Wumpus{1,3}\) for "The Wumpus is in square [1,3]", \(\BreezeProp{1,1}\) for "Square [1,1] is breezy"). Using meaningful names improves readability for humans.

  • The two constant symbols \(\texttt{True}\) (representing the proposition that is always true) and \(\texttt{False}\) (representing the proposition that is always false) are also considered atomic sentences.

Logical Connectives

Complex sentences are constructed by combining simpler sentences using five standard logical connectives:

  • \(\neg\) : Negation (NOT). \(\neg P\) ("not P") is true if and only if \(P\) is false.

  • \(\land\) : Conjunction (AND). \(P \land Q\) ("P and Q") is true if and only if both \(P\) and \(Q\) are true.

  • \(\lor\) : Disjunction (OR). \(P \lor Q\) ("P or Q") is true if and only if at least one of \(P\) or \(Q\) is true. This is an inclusive OR.

  • \(\implies\) : Implication (Conditional, IF...THEN...). \(P \implies Q\) ("P implies Q" or "if P then Q") is true unless \(P\) is true and \(Q\) is false. \(P\) is called the antecedent (or premise), and \(Q\) is the consequent (or conclusion).

  • \(\iff\) : Biconditional (Equivalence, IF AND ONLY IF). \(P \iff Q\) ("P if and only if Q") is true if and only if \(P\) and \(Q\) have the same truth value (both true or both false).

Formal Grammar (BNF)

The set of all well-formed sentences in PL can be defined recursively using Backus-Naur Form (BNF): \[\begin{aligned} \textbf{Sentence} &\to \textbf{AtomicSentence} \mid \textbf{ComplexSentence} \\ \textbf{AtomicSentence} &\to \texttt{True}\mid \texttt{False}\mid P \mid Q \mid R \mid \dots \mid P_{1,1,\mid }\dots \\ \textbf{ComplexSentence} &\to (\textbf{Sentence}) && \text{Parenthesized sentence} \\ &\mid \neg \textbf{Sentence} && \text{Negation} \\ &\mid \textbf{Sentence} \land \textbf{Sentence} && \text{Conjunction} \\ &\mid \textbf{Sentence} \lor \textbf{Sentence} && \text{Disjunction} \\ &\mid \textbf{Sentence} \implies \textbf{Sentence} && \text{Implication} \\ &\mid \textbf{Sentence} \iff \textbf{Sentence} && \text{Biconditional}\end{aligned}\] This grammar ensures that sentences are constructed unambiguously. Parentheses are crucial for defining the scope of connectives.

Operator Precedence

To minimize the use of parentheses and improve readability, a standard order of precedence is defined for the connectives:

  1. \(\neg\) (highest precedence)

  2. \(\land\)

  3. \(\lor\)

  4. \(\implies\)

  5. \(\iff\) (lowest precedence)

Connectives are typically assumed to be left-associative when precedence is equal (e.g., \(A \land B \land C\) means \((A \land B) \land C\)).

Example : The sentence \(\neg P \land Q \lor R \implies S\) is parsed according to precedence as \((((\neg P) \land Q) \lor R) \implies S\).

Semantics

Semantics defines the meaning of PL sentences by specifying how to determine their truth value.

Truth Assignments (Models)

The core idea is to assign a truth value (True or False) to each atomic propositional symbol.

Definition : Model in PL A model \(m\) in propositional logic is a complete assignment of truth values to all propositional symbols in the language. If a language uses symbols \(P_1, P_2, \dots, P_n\), a model specifies a value (\({\texttt{True}}\) or \({\texttt{False}}\)) for each \(P_i\). A model represents one possible way the world could be, concerning the truth of the basic propositions.

Example : If the only symbols are \(P, Q, R\), then \(m = \{P=\texttt{True}, Q=\texttt{False}, R=\texttt{True}\}\) is one possible model. There are \(2^3 = 8\) possible models in this case. In general, for \(n\) propositional symbols, there are \(2^n\) distinct models.

Truth Tables for Connectives

Once truth values are assigned to atomic sentences by a model, the truth value of any complex sentence can be determined recursively using the standard definitions of the connectives, often summarized in truth tables:

\(P\) \(Q\) \(\neg P\) \(P \land Q\) \(P \lor Q\) \(P \implies Q\) \(P \iff Q\)
False False True False False True True
False True True False True True False
True False False False True False False
True True False True True True True

Semantics of Implication (Material Implication)

The definition of \(P \implies Q\) warrants special attention. It is known as material implication.

  • It is defined to be equivalent to \(\neg P \lor Q\).

  • It is only False when the antecedent (\(P\)) is True and the consequent (\(Q\)) is False.

  • In all other cases, it is True. This includes cases where the antecedent is false (\(P\) is False), regardless of the truth value of the consequent (\(Q\)). This property is sometimes summarized as "ex falso quodlibet" (from falsehood, anything follows).

  • It’s crucial to distinguish material implication from causation or relevance in natural language. \(P \implies Q\) does not mean \(P\) causes \(Q\), only that it’s not the case that \(P\) is true while \(Q\) is false.

Example : The sentence "If the moon is made of green cheese, then \(2+2=5\)" is considered True under material implication, because the antecedent ("the moon is made of green cheese") is false.

Relation between Implication and Entailment (Deduction Theorem)

There’s a fundamental link between the syntactic connective \(\implies\) and the semantic notion of entailment \(\models\).

Theorem : Deduction Theorem for PL For any sentences \(\alpha\) and \(\beta\), \(\alpha \models \beta\) if and only if the sentence \((\alpha \implies \beta)\) is valid (i.e., true in all possible models). This theorem connects the semantic concept of entailment (truth preservation across all models) with the syntactic concept of implication and the semantic property of validity (truth in all models). It provides a method to check entailment by checking the validity of a single implication sentence.

This theorem provides a bridge between checking entailment (a semantic task involving potentially many models) and proving the validity of a single implication sentence (which can sometimes be done syntactically). It highlights that entailment captures the essence of logical implication: if \(\alpha\) entails \(\beta\), then the truth of \(\alpha\) guarantees the truth of \(\beta\).

Satisfiability, Validity, and Unsatisfiability

Based on the evaluation of sentences in models, we can define important semantic properties:

  • Satisfiability: A sentence \(\alpha\) is satisfiable if it is true in at least one model.

  • Validity (Tautology): A sentence \(\alpha\) is valid (or a tautology) if it is true in all possible models (e.g., \(P \lor \neg P\)). Valid sentences are necessarily true based on their logical structure alone.

  • Unsatisfiability (Contradiction): A sentence \(\alpha\) is unsatisfiable (or a contradiction) if it is false in all possible models (e.g., \(P \land \neg P\)).

These concepts are related to entailment:

  • \(\textit{KB}{} \models \beta\) if and only if \((\textit{KB}{} \implies \beta)\) is valid (Deduction Theorem, where \(\textit{KB}{}\) is treated as the conjunction of its sentences).

  • \(\textit{KB}{} \models \beta\) if and only if \((\textit{KB}{} \land \neg \beta)\) is unsatisfiable. This forms the basis for proof by contradiction or refutation methods in inference.

Inference in Propositional Logic

Having defined the syntax and semantics of propositional logic, we now turn to inference: the process of deriving new sentences (conclusions) from existing ones (premises in the KB). We focus primarily on the syntactic approach, known as theorem proving, which relies on the application of inference rules.

Inference as Search

The task of determining whether \(\textit{KB}{} \models \beta\) (or finding \(\beta\) such that \(\textit{KB}{} \models \beta\)) can be framed as a search problem.

  • States: Represent the set of sentences currently known or derived. The initial state contains only the sentences in the original KB.

  • Actions/Operators: Applications of sound inference rules. Applying a rule to sentences in the current state generates a new sentence, leading to a successor state that includes the new sentence.

  • Goal State: A state that contains the target sentence \(\beta\) (if we are trying to prove a specific \(\beta\)), or a state where no new sentences can be derived (if we are trying to find all consequences).

  • Search Strategies: Standard search algorithms can be adapted.

    • Forward Chaining (Data-Driven): Start with the KB and repeatedly apply inference rules to derive all possible conclusions until the goal sentence is found or no more inferences can be made.

    • Backward Chaining (Goal-Driven): Start with the goal sentence \(\beta\) and work backward. To prove \(\beta\), find rules whose conclusion is \(\beta\) and try to prove their premises. This is often more focused if the goal is known.

Remark : It is crucial to understand that this search occurs in the space of logical derivations, manipulating sentences based on inference rules. It is distinct from searching the state space of the agent’s environment (e.g., the grid locations in the Wumpus World). We are searching for a proof, not a physical path for the agent.

Inference Rules

Inference rules are patterns that specify how to derive valid conclusions from a set of premises. They are designed to be sound, meaning they only generate conclusions that are entailed by the premises.

An inference rule is typically written as: \[\frac{\text{Premise}_1, \quad \text{Premise}_2, \quad \dots}{\text{Conclusion}}\] This means that if all the premises above the line are known to be true, then the conclusion below the line can be validly inferred.

Here are some fundamental inference rules for propositional logic:

Modus Ponens (MP)

Latin for "mode that affirms". This is perhaps the most famous inference rule. \[\frac{\alpha, \quad \alpha \implies \beta}{\beta} \quad (\text{Modus Ponens})\] If we know an implication (\(\alpha \implies \beta\)) holds and its antecedent (\(\alpha\)) is true, we can infer that the consequent (\(\beta\)) is true. This rule is central to forward chaining.

Modus Tollens (MT)

Latin for "mode that denies". It works with the contrapositive form of implication. \[\frac{\alpha \implies \beta, \quad \neg \beta}{\neg \alpha} \quad (\text{Modus Tollens})\] If we know an implication holds and its consequent (\(\beta\)) is false, we can infer that the antecedent (\(\alpha\)) must be false.

And Elimination (\(\land\) E)

Also known as Simplification. From a conjunction, we can infer any of its individual conjuncts. \[\frac{\alpha_1 \land \alpha_2 \land \dots \land \alpha_n}{\alpha_i} \quad (\text{And Elimination})\] For example, from \(P \land Q\), we can infer \(P\). We can also infer \(Q\).

And Introduction (\(\land\) I)

Also known as Conjunction. If we know several sentences are true individually, we can infer their conjunction. \[\frac{\alpha_1, \quad \alpha_2, \quad \dots, \quad \alpha_n}{\alpha_1 \land \alpha_2 \land \dots \land \alpha_n} \quad (\text{And Introduction})\] For example, if we know \(P\) and we know \(Q\), we can infer \(P \land Q\).

Or Introduction (\(\lor\) I)

Also known as Addition. If we know a sentence is true, we can infer its disjunction with any other sentence. \[\frac{\alpha_i}{\alpha_1 \lor \alpha_2 \lor \dots \lor \alpha_n} \quad (\text{Or Introduction})\] For example, if we know \(P\), we can infer \(P \lor Q\) (for any \(Q\)).

Resolution

Resolution is a single, powerful inference rule that is sound and, importantly, refutation-complete for propositional logic when sentences are converted into a specific format called Conjunctive Normal Form (CNF).

Preliminaries: Literals and Clauses

  • A literal is an atomic sentence (a positive literal, e.g., \(P\)) or the negation of an atomic sentence (a negative literal, e.g., \(\neg P\)).

  • A clause is a disjunction of one or more literals (e.g., \(P \lor \neg Q \lor R\)). A single literal is also considered a clause.

  • Conjunctive Normal Form (CNF): A sentence is in CNF if it is a conjunction of clauses. Example: \((P \lor \neg Q) \land (\neg P \lor R) \land Q\). Any propositional logic sentence can be converted into an equivalent CNF sentence (though the conversion might increase the sentence size). Resolution typically operates on sentences in CNF.

The Resolution Rule:

The rule works by identifying two clauses containing complementary literals (e.g., \(P\) in one clause and \(\neg P\) in another) and producing a new clause (the resolvent) containing all literals from both original clauses except the complementary pair.

  • Unit Resolution: A special case where one clause is just a single literal (a unit clause). \[\frac{l_1 \lor \dots \lor l_k, \quad \neg l_i}{l_1 \lor \dots \lor l_{i-1} \lor l_{i+1} \lor \dots \lor l_k} \quad (\text{Unit Resolution})\] If we have a disjunction and know that one of the disjuncts (\(l_i\)) is false (i.e., we know \(\neg l_i\)), we can remove \(l_i\) from the clause.

  • Generalized Resolution Rule: The general form operates on any two clauses. \[\frac{l_1 \lor \dots \lor l_i \lor \dots \lor l_k, \quad m_1 \lor \dots \lor \neg l_i \lor \dots \lor m_n}{(l_1 \lor \dots \lor l_{i-1} \lor l_{i+1} \lor \dots \lor l_k) \lor (m_1 \lor \dots \lor m_{j-1} \lor m_{j+1} \lor \dots \lor m_n)} \quad (\text{Resolution})\] where \(l_i\) is a literal appearing in the first clause, and \(\neg l_i\) is its complement appearing in the second clause. The resulting clause contains all literals from the original clauses except for the complementary pair \(l_i\) and \(\neg l_i\). If the resolvent contains both \(L\) and \(\neg L\) for some literal \(L\), it is a tautology and can be discarded. If the resolvent contains duplicate literals, they can be merged (e.g., \(A \lor B \lor A \equiv A \lor B\)). If resolution produces an empty clause (containing no literals), this represents \(\texttt{False}\) and signifies a contradiction in the original set of clauses.

Intuition behind Resolution Consider the premises \(A \lor X\) and \(B \lor \neg X\). We want to show they entail \(A \lor B\). We can reason by cases based on the truth value of \(X\):

  • Case 1: Assume \(X\) is True. For the second premise \(B \lor \neg X\) to be true, since \(\neg X\) is False, \(B\) must be True. If \(B\) is True, then \(A \lor B\) must be True.

  • Case 2: Assume \(X\) is False. For the first premise \(A \lor X\) to be true, since \(X\) is False, \(A\) must be True. If \(A\) is True, then \(A \lor B\) must be True.

Since \(X\) must be either True or False, and in both cases \(A \lor B\) follows, we can conclude \(A \lor B\). The resolution rule captures this case analysis syntactically. \[\frac{A \lor X, \quad B \lor \neg X}{A \lor B}\] This also relates to implication: \(A \lor X \equiv \neg A \implies X\) and \(B \lor \neg X \equiv X \implies B\). Resolution acts like a generalized form of transitivity: from \(\neg A \implies X\) and \(X \implies B\), we get \(\neg A \implies B\), which is equivalent to \(A \lor B\).

Resolution Example in Wumpus World Recall the scenario: Agent in [2,1] perceives breeze. Knows \(\neg \Pit{1,1}\). Rule: \(B_{2,1,\iff }(P_{1,1,\lor }P_{2,2,\lor }P_{3,1,)}\).

  1. Convert knowledge to CNF clauses:

    • From \(\BreezeProp{2,1}\) and the rule (using biconditional elimination, implication elimination): \(P_{1,1,\lor }P_{2,2,\lor }\Pit{3,1}\) (Clause \(C_1\))

    • From known fact: \(\neg \Pit{1,1}\) (Clause \(C_2\))

  2. Apply Resolution: Resolve \(C_1\) and \(C_2\) on the complementary literals \(\Pit{1,1}\) and \(\neg \Pit{1,1}\). \[\frac{P_{1,1,\lor }P_{2,2,\lor }P_{3,1,,} \quad \neg \Pit{1,1}}{P_{2,2,\lor }\Pit{3,1}}\] Resulting clause \(C_3: P_{2,2,\lor }\Pit{3,1}\). This means there must be a pit in [2,2] or [3,1].

  3. Incorporate new knowledge: Suppose the agent later visits [1,2] and perceives no breeze. This implies \(\neg \BreezeProp{1,2}\). The rule is \(B_{1,2,\iff }(P_{1,1,\lor }P_{2,2,\lor }P_{1,3,)}\).

    • From \(\neg \BreezeProp{1,2}\) and the rule: \(\neg (P_{1,1,\lor }P_{2,2,\lor }P_{1,3,)}\)

    • Applying De Morgan’s Law: \(\neg P_{1,1,\land }\neg P_{2,2,\land }\neg \Pit{1,3}\)

    • Using And Elimination, we get three unit clauses, including: \(\neg \Pit{2,2}\) (Clause \(C_4\))

  4. Apply Resolution again: Resolve \(C_3\) and \(C_4\) on \(\Pit{2,2}\) and \(\neg \Pit{2,2}\). \[\frac{P_{2,2,\lor }P_{3,1,,} \quad \neg \Pit{2,2}}{\Pit{3,1}}\] Resulting clause \(C_5: \Pit{3,1}\). The agent has successfully inferred the exact location of a pit in [3,1].

Refutation Completeness:

Resolution’s key property is refutation completeness. This means:

  • If a set of clauses \(S\) is unsatisfiable (contains a contradiction), then repeated application of the resolution rule is guaranteed to eventually derive the empty clause (\(\texttt{False}\)).

  • This allows us to prove entailment \(\textit{KB}{} \models \alpha\) using proof by contradiction:

    1. Add the negation of the goal, \(\neg \alpha\), to the knowledge base \(\textit{KB}{}\).

    2. Convert all sentences in \(\textit{KB}{} \cup \{\neg \alpha\}\) into CNF.

    3. Apply the resolution rule repeatedly to the set of clauses.

    4. If the empty clause is derived, the original set \(\textit{KB}{} \cup \{\neg \alpha\}\) is unsatisfiable, which means \(\textit{KB}{}\) must entail \(\alpha\).

    5. If the empty clause cannot be derived and no new clauses can be generated, then \(\textit{KB}{}\) does not entail \(\alpha\).

Remark : While resolution is complete, the process of deriving the empty clause (or determining that it cannot be derived) can take time exponential in the number of symbols in the worst case. However, many heuristics and optimized implementations (like those used in modern SAT solvers based on the DPLL algorithm, which incorporates resolution) make it practical for many problems.

Common Logical Equivalences

Logical equivalences are pairs of sentences that have the same truth value in all models (\(\alpha \equiv \beta\) means \(\alpha \models \beta\) and \(\beta \models \alpha\)). They are not inference rules themselves, but are crucial tools for rewriting sentences into simpler or standard forms (like CNF) without changing their meaning. Applying equivalences is often a preprocessing step before using inference rules like resolution.

Key equivalences include:

  • Commutativity: \((\alpha \land \beta) \equiv (\beta \land \alpha)\); \((\alpha \lor \beta) \equiv (\beta \lor \alpha)\)

  • Associativity: \(((\alpha \land \beta) \land \gamma) \equiv (\alpha \land (\beta \land \gamma))\); \(((\alpha \lor \beta) \lor \gamma) \equiv (\alpha \lor (\beta \lor \gamma))\)

  • Idempotence: \((\alpha \land \alpha) \equiv \alpha\); \((\alpha \lor \alpha) \equiv \alpha\)

  • Double Negation Elimination: \(\neg (\neg \alpha) \equiv \alpha\)

  • Contraposition: \((\alpha \implies \beta) \equiv (\neg \beta \implies \neg \alpha)\)

  • Implication Elimination: \((\alpha \implies \beta) \equiv (\neg \alpha \lor \beta)\)

  • Biconditional Elimination: \((\alpha \iff \beta) \equiv ((\alpha \implies \beta) \land (\beta \implies \alpha)) \equiv ((\neg \alpha \lor \beta) \land (\neg \beta \lor \alpha))\)

  • De Morgan’s Laws: \(\neg (\alpha \land \beta) \equiv (\neg \alpha \lor \neg \beta)\); \(\neg (\alpha \lor \beta) \equiv (\neg \alpha \land \neg \beta)\)

  • Distributivity: \((\alpha \land (\beta \lor \gamma)) \equiv ((\alpha \land \beta) \lor (\alpha \land \gamma))\); \((\alpha \lor (\beta \land \gamma)) \equiv ((\alpha \lor \beta) \land (\alpha \lor \gamma))\)

  • Absorption Laws: \((\alpha \land (\alpha \lor \beta)) \equiv \alpha\); \((\alpha \lor (\alpha \land \beta)) \equiv \alpha\)

(And others involving \(\texttt{True}\) and \(\texttt{False}\)). These equivalences form the basis of algebraic manipulation of logical sentences.

Example Proof using Inference Rules and Equivalences

Let’s formally derive \(\neg \Pit{1,2}\) (Square [1,2] is safe from pits) given the agent’s initial knowledge in the Wumpus World, demonstrating the application of various rules.

Knowledge Base (KB):

  • \(R_1: \neg \Pit{1,1}\) (Game rule/Initial state)

  • \(R_2: B_{1,1,\iff }(P_{1,2,\lor }P_{2,1,)}\) (Physics rule for [1,1])

  • \(R_3: \neg \BreezeProp{1,1}\) (Percept at [1,1] at time 0)

Goal:

Prove \(\neg \Pit{1,2}\).

Proof. Derivation using Inference Rules. We start with the premises in the KB and apply rules and equivalences step-by-step.

  1. \(B_{1,1,\iff }(P_{1,2,\lor }P_{2,1,)}\) (Premise \(R_2\))

  2. \((B_{1,1,\implies }(P_{1,2,\lor }P_{2,1,)}) \land ((P_{1,2,\lor }P_{2,1,)} \implies B_{1,1,)}\) (Biconditional Elimination on 1)

  3. \((P_{1,2,\lor }P_{2,1,)} \implies \BreezeProp{1,1}\) (And Elimination (\(\land\) E) on 2)

  4. \(\neg B_{1,1,\implies }\neg (P_{1,2,\lor }P_{2,1,)}\) (Contraposition on 3)

  5. \(\neg \BreezeProp{1,1}\) (Premise \(R_3\))

  6. \(\neg (P_{1,2,\lor }P_{2,1,)}\) (Modus Ponens (MP) on 4 and 5)

  7. \(\neg P_{1,2,\land }\neg \Pit{2,1}\) (De Morgan’s Law on 6)

  8. \(\neg \Pit{1,2}\) (And Elimination (\(\land\) E) on 7)

The derivation successfully concludes \(\neg \Pit{1,2}\), demonstrating that square [1,2] is provably safe based on the initial knowledge and percepts. This sequence of steps constitutes a formal proof within the system of propositional logic. ◻

A Propositional Logic Agent for the Wumpus World

We now apply the concepts of propositional logic to design a knowledge-based agent capable of reasoning and acting in the Wumpus World. This involves constructing a suitable Knowledge Base (KB) and understanding how the agent uses inference. This process also reveals significant challenges inherent in using propositional logic for dynamic environments.

Building the Knowledge Base (KB)

The KB must encode the agent’s knowledge about the Wumpus World, including static rules and dynamic information.

Initial State and Static Rules (Atemporal Knowledge)

These are facts and rules that hold true throughout the agent’s existence in the environment.

  • Initial State Facts: The agent starts in [1,1], which is known to be safe.

    • \(\neg \Pit{1,1}\) (No pit in [1,1])

    • \(\neg \Wumpus{1,1}\) (No Wumpus in [1,1])

  • Environment Physics (Percept Rules): These rules define the relationship between hazards (pits, Wumpus) and percepts (breeze, stench). They must be specified for every square.

    • For square [1,1]:

      • \(B_{1,1,\iff }(P_{1,2,\lor }P_{2,1,)}\) (Square [1,1] is breezy iff [1,2] or [2,1] has a pit)

      • \(S_{1,1,\iff }(W_{1,2,\lor }W_{2,1,)}\) (Square [1,1] is stinky iff [1,2] or [2,1] has the Wumpus)

    • For square [1,2]:

      • \(B_{1,2,\iff }(P_{1,1,\lor }P_{2,2,\lor }P_{1,3,)}\)

      • \(S_{1,2,\iff }(W_{1,1,\lor }W_{2,2,\lor }W_{1,3,)}\)

    • ... and so on for all 16 squares.

    Note that \(\Pit{x,y}\), \(\Wumpus{x,y}\), \(\BreezeProp{x,y}\), and \(\StenchProp{x,y}\) represent static properties of the squares themselves.

  • Size Implication: Writing these rules explicitly requires \(16 \times 2 = 32\) biconditional sentences, each potentially expanding into multiple clauses in CNF. This is manageable but already hints at scalability issues.

Representing Uniqueness Constraints

The rules state there is exactly one Wumpus. This needs to be encoded explicitly.

Encoding such constraints, while possible, significantly increases the size of the KB. Similar constraints might be needed for the gold, though typically not for pits (which can be multiple).

Handling Time and Change

The Wumpus World is dynamic from the agent’s perspective: its location changes, its percepts change, the Wumpus’s status might change (alive to dead), and its possession of the arrow changes. Representing this change within propositional logic poses a challenge.

The Problem with Static Symbols for Dynamic Properties

If we try to use static propositional symbols for properties that change over time (like the agent’s percepts or location), we run into contradictions.

Example : Contradiction with Static Percept Symbol Suppose we use a single symbol \(Stench\) to mean "The agent currently perceives a stench".

  • Time \(t=4\): Agent enters a square adjacent to the Wumpus and perceives a stench. We add \(Stench\) to the KB.

  • Time \(t=5\): Agent moves away and no longer perceives a stench. We add \(\neg Stench\) to the KB.

  • Problem: The KB now contains both \(Stench\) and \(\neg Stench\). This is a logical contradiction (\(P \land \neg P\)). From a contradiction, any arbitrary sentence can be derived (via the principle of explosion, \(P \land \neg P \models Q\)), rendering the KB useless for sound reasoning.

Introducing Time Indices

The standard solution in propositional logic is to make propositions time-dependent by adding a time index (often as a superscript) to symbols representing properties that change.

  • \(Stench^{4}\): The agent perceives a stench at time \(t=4\).

  • \(Breeze^{5}\): The agent perceives a breeze at time \(t=5\).

  • \(\neg Stench^{5}\): The agent does not perceive a stench at time \(t=5\).

Now, \(Stench^{4}\) and \(\neg Stench^{5}\) are distinct propositional symbols. They can coexist in the KB without causing a contradiction, correctly reflecting the change in perception over time.

Fluents: Time-Varying Propositions

Symbols representing properties that can change their truth value over time are called fluents.

  • Fluent Examples:

    • \(L_{x,y}^{t}\): Agent is at location \((x,y)\) at time \(t\). (e.g., \(L_{1,1}^{0}\))

    • \(FacingEast^{t}\): Agent is facing East at time \(t\). (Similarly \(\mathit{FacingNorth}^t\), etc.)

    • \(HaveArrow^{t}\): Agent possesses the arrow at time \(t\).

    • \(WumpusAlive^{t}\): The Wumpus is alive at time \(t\).

    • \(Breeze^{t}\), \(Stench^{t}\), \(\mathit{Glitter}^t\), \(\mathit{Bump}^t\), \(\mathit{Scream}^t\): Percept symbols at time \(t\).

  • Atemporal (Non-Fluent) Symbols: Represent static properties of the environment that do not change over time.

    • \(\Pit{x,y}\): There is a pit in square \((x,y)\).

    • \(\Wumpus{x,y}\): The Wumpus (initially) is in square \((x,y)\). (Note: \(WumpusAlive^{t}\) is the fluent tracking if it’s currently alive).

    • \(\BreezeProp{x,y}\), \(\StenchProp{x,y}\): The intrinsic property of square \((x,y)\) being breezy or stinky (due to adjacent hazards).

This explicit representation of time via indices allows PL to handle dynamic aspects, but at the cost of proliferating symbols for each time step.

Connecting Percepts to State across Time

The agent’s percepts at a given time depend on its state (location) and the static properties of the environment at that location. This relationship must be encoded in the KB. Example: "If the agent is at (x,y) at time t, then it perceives a breeze at time t if and only if the square (x,y) has the intrinsic breeze property." \[\forall x,y,t: \quad L_{x,y}^{t} \implies (Breeze^{t} \iff B_{x,y,)}\] Since PL cannot express quantification (\(\forall\)), this must be written out as separate axioms for each square \((x,y)\) and potentially each relevant time step \(t\). \[L_{1,1}^{t} \implies (Breeze^{t} \iff B_{1,1,)}\] \[L_{1,2}^{t} \implies (Breeze^{t} \iff B_{1,2,)}\] \[\dots\] Similar rules are needed for stench and glitter. This further increases the size of the KB, especially as the time horizon grows.

Representing Actions and State Transitions

To reason about the future and plan sequences of actions, the agent needs knowledge about how actions affect the state of the world (i.e., the truth values of fluents). This requires a transition model.

Need for a Transition Model

The transition model describes how the world state at time \(t+1\) depends on the state at time \(t\) and the action performed at time \(t\). In PL with time indices, this is typically encoded using successor-state axioms.

Successor-State Axioms

A successor-state axiom defines the truth value of a fluent at time \(t+1\) based on the state and action at time \(t\).

Example : Effect of Forward Action on Location Consider the fluent \(L_{2,1}^{t+1}\) (being at [2,1] at time \(t+1\)). When is this true? It’s true if the agent was at [1,1] facing East at time \(t\) and moved Forward, OR if it was at [2,2] facing South at \(t\) and moved Forward, OR ... A simplified axiom focusing only on one cause: "If the agent is at [1,1] at time \(t\), facing East, and performs the \(Forward\) action at time \(t\), then at time \(t+1\) the agent will be at [2,1]." \[(L_{1,1}^{t} \land FacingEast^{t} \land Forward^{t}) \implies L_{2,1}^{t+1}\] This captures the positive effect of the action. We also need to capture negative effects: "If the agent is at [1,1] at time \(t\), facing East, and performs the \(Forward\) action at time \(t\), then at time \(t+1\) the agent will not be at [1,1]." \[(L_{1,1}^{t} \land FacingEast^{t} \land Forward^{t}) \implies \neg L_{1,1}^{t+1}\]

Completeness Requirement:

To fully specify the transition model, such axioms must cover:

  • All possible actions (\(Forward, TurnRight, TurnLeft, Grab, Shoot, Climb\)). We need symbols like \(Forward^{t}, TurnRight^{t}, \dots\) to represent the action performed at time \(t\).

  • All fluents that can be affected by actions.

  • All conditions under which a fluent’s value changes.

  • Crucially, all conditions under which a fluent’s value does not change (this leads to the Frame Problem, see 5.5).

Writing a complete set of successor-state axioms in PL becomes extremely verbose, requiring axioms for every fluent, every action, and every time step considered.

Reasoning with the Temporal KB

With the temporal KB constructed, the agent can perform inference over time.

Positive Inference Example (Inferring New Location)

The agent can use inference rules like Modus Ponens to predict the outcome of its actions.

  • Assume KB contains at time \(t=0\):

    • \(L_{1,1}^{0}\) (Fact)

    • \(FacingEast^{0}\) (Fact)

    • \(Forward^{0}\) (Agent decides to execute Forward action)

    • \((L_{1,1}^{0} \land FacingEast^{0} \land Forward^{0}) \implies L_{2,1}^{1}\) (Instance of successor-state axiom)

  • Inference: By applying Modus Ponens to these sentences, the agent can derive: \[L_{2,1}^{1}\]

The agent successfully deduces its location at the next time step, \(t=1\).

The Challenge: Inferring What *Doesn’t* Change

A significant difficulty arises when trying to infer that properties remain the same after an action, if that action doesn’t explicitly affect them.

Example : Inferring Arrow Possession

  • Assume KB contains at time \(t=0\):

    • \(HaveArrow^{0}\) (Agent starts with the arrow)

    • \(Forward^{0}\) (Agent performs Forward action)

    • Successor-state axioms for location (as above).

  • Query: Does the agent have the arrow at time \(t=1\)? (\(\textit{KB}{} \models HaveArrow^{1}\)?)

  • Problem: Based only on the axioms provided so far (which describe location changes), the answer is no. There is no axiom stating that the \(Forward\) action preserves the arrow. The KB does not contain enough information to conclude \(HaveArrow^{1}\).

  • Worse: The agent also cannot conclude \(\neg HaveArrow^{1}\). It simply remains ignorant about the arrow’s status at \(t=1\).

This seems counter-intuitive; common sense suggests that moving forward shouldn’t affect whether the agent holds an arrow. However, logic requires explicit statements.

Limitation: The Frame Problem

The difficulty in representing and reasoning about properties that do not change after an action is known as the Frame Problem.

The Frame Problem

Definition : Frame Problem The Frame Problem is the challenge of concisely and efficiently representing the non-effects of actions. In a logical framework like PL with successor-state axioms, it manifests as the need to explicitly state all the fluents that remain unchanged by each action. Humans naturally assume inertia: things tend to stay the same unless explicitly acted upon. Representing this assumption efficiently in logic is difficult.

Illustration (HasArrow Example)

To allow the agent to correctly infer \(HaveArrow^{1}\) after the \(Forward^{0}\) action, the KB must include explicit frame axioms that state the conditions under which the arrow status persists. Example Frame Axiom for \(\HaveArrow\): "If the agent has the arrow at time \(t\) and the action performed at time \(t\) is not \(Shoot\), then the agent still has the arrow at time \(t+1\)." \[(HaveArrow^{t} \land \neg Shoot^{t}) \implies HaveArrow^{t+1}\] (A more complete axiom would list all actions that don’t affect the arrow).

Implications for KB Size and Representational Complexity

  • Axiom Proliferation: Frame axioms must be specified for nearly every combination of fluent and action that does not cause a change. If there are \(F\) fluents and \(A\) actions, the number of frame axioms can be roughly proportional to \(F \times A\).

  • KB Size: In the Wumpus World, with potentially dozens of location fluents, orientation fluents, \(\HaveArrow\), \(\WumpusAlive\), etc., and several actions, the number of frame axioms becomes enormous, dwarfing the number of effect axioms.

  • Manageability: Constructing and maintaining such a large KB is tedious and error-prone.

  • Inference Cost: Inference algorithms become significantly slower when operating on a vastly larger set of axioms.

The Frame Problem was a major obstacle identified in early AI research, highlighting a fundamental limitation of using simple logical representations for dynamic worlds. It suggested that purely deductive reasoning based on explicitly stated effects and non-effects might not scale effectively.

Note : While initially seen as a potentially fatal flaw for logic-based AI, various solutions and alternative formalisms (e.g., non-monotonic logics, situation calculus with default assumptions, STRIPS representations) have been developed to address the Frame Problem more elegantly than explicitly listing all frame axioms. However, it remains a key conceptual challenge in knowledge representation and reasoning about action and change.

Conclusion and Outlook

This lecture introduced propositional logic as a formal framework for enabling knowledge representation and reasoning in intelligent agents. We explored its fundamental components and applied them to the Wumpus World scenario.

Summary: Propositional Logic for Knowledge-Based Agents

  • Formal Representation: Propositional Logic (PL) provides a well-defined syntax for expressing facts and rules as sentences, and clear semantics based on truth assignments in models.

  • Inference Capabilities: Agents can employ logical inference procedures (either semantic model checking or syntactic theorem proving using rules like Modus Ponens and Resolution) to derive new knowledge (logical consequences) from their KB, going beyond explicitly stored information.

  • Wumpus World KB Construction: We outlined the process of building a KB for the Wumpus World agent, encoding:

    • Static knowledge: Initial state facts, environment physics (percept rules), uniqueness constraints.

    • Dynamic knowledge: Using time indices (superscripts) to create fluents for representing time-varying properties (location, percepts, agent status).

    • Action effects: Using successor-state axioms to model how actions change the state (fluents) over time.

  • Reasoning Over Time: The temporal KB allows the agent to reason about the effects of its actions and make predictions about future states.

Key Challenge: The Frame Problem

Despite its formal clarity, applying PL to dynamic environments like the Wumpus World reveals a major challenge:

  • Representing Non-Change: PL requires explicit statements not only about what changes due to an action (effect axioms) but also about everything that remains unchanged (frame axioms).

  • Axiom Explosion: The need for numerous frame axioms leads to an explosion in the size and complexity of the KB, making representation cumbersome and inference potentially inefficient.

  • Scalability Impact: This Frame Problem significantly hinders the scalability and practical application of pure propositional logic for representing knowledge in complex, dynamic domains where most properties persist over time.

Preview: Addressing Limitations and Moving to First-Order Logic

The limitations encountered, particularly the representational inefficiency stemming from the atomic nature of propositions and the Frame Problem, motivate the exploration of more powerful logical frameworks.

  • Addressing the Frame Problem: While solutions exist within logical frameworks (e.g., assuming inertia by default, specialized action representations), the inherent verbosity of PL remains.

  • Next Steps: First-Order Logic (FOL): The next lectures will introduce First-Order Logic (FOL), also known as predicate calculus. FOL offers significantly greater expressive power by incorporating objects, relations, variables, and quantifiers (\(\forall, \exists\)). This allows for more compact and natural representation of general rules (e.g., "All squares adjacent to a pit are breezy") and potentially more elegant ways to handle reasoning about action and change, mitigating some of the representational burdens encountered in PL.

# Introduction {#sec:introduction}

This lecture delves into the use of *propositional logic* as a
foundational tool for building more intelligent agents. We explore how
this formal language allows agents to represent knowledge explicitly and
perform logical reasoning, moving beyond the capabilities of simpler
reflex or goal-based agents discussed previously.

## Recap: Knowledge-Based Agents {#sec:recap_kb_agents}

We previously introduced the paradigm of *knowledge-based agents*. These
agents maintain an internal **Knowledge Base (*KB*)**, which is a
collection of sentences expressed in a formal language, representing
what the agent knows about the world. The core components and cycle of
operation involve:

-   A **Knowledge Base (*KB*)**: Stores facts and rules about the
    environment. Initially, it contains background knowledge; later, it
    incorporates information derived from percepts.

-   An **Inference Mechanism (Engine)**: A computational process that
    derives new sentences (knowledge) logically implied by the sentences
    already in the *KB*.

-   A **Tell** operation: Used to add new sentences to the *KB*,
    typically derived from the agent's perceptions of the environment.

-   An **Ask** operation: Used to query the *KB*. The agent asks what
    actions are appropriate or what the properties of the world are,
    based on its current knowledge and inferred conclusions.

The agent's intelligence and ability to act rationally depend crucially
on the richness of its *KB* and the capabilities of its inference
mechanism. Propositional logic provides a concrete framework for
implementing both the representation (*KB*) and the reasoning
(`Ask`/`Tell` involving inference).

## The Wumpus World Revisited {#sec:wumpus_revisited}

We continue to use the **Wumpus World** as a canonical example
environment for knowledge-based agents. It serves as an effective
testbed because success requires non-trivial reasoning about hidden
aspects of the environment.

#### Agent's Goal and Performance Measure:

The agent starts at square \[1,1\], facing East. The goal is to navigate
the grid, find the gold, return to \[1,1\], and execute the 'Climb'
action to exit. The performance measure typically involves:

-   +1000 points for climbing out with the gold.

-   -1000 points for falling into a pit or being eaten by the Wumpus
    (death).

-   -10 points for using the arrow.

-   -1 point for each action taken (encouraging efficiency).

### Task Environment Characteristics (PEAS Analysis)

Analyzing the Wumpus World according to the standard dimensions helps
clarify the challenges the agent faces:

-   **Observability: Partially Observable.** The agent's sensors provide
    only local information: stench (adjacent Wumpus), breeze (adjacent
    pit), glitter (gold in current square), bump (hit a wall), scream
    (Wumpus killed). Crucial information is not directly sensed:

    -   The agent's absolute location must be tracked internally based
        on movement actions.

    -   Whether the Wumpus is alive or dead must be inferred from
        hearing (or not hearing) the scream after shooting.

    -   Whether the agent possesses the arrow must be tracked internally
        after a 'Shoot' action.

    This partial observability necessitates maintaining an internal
    state representing beliefs about the unobserved aspects of the
    world.

-   **Agents: Single Agent.** Although the Wumpus and pits pose threats,
    they are static elements of the environment. They do not act
    independently or strategically. The only active entity changing the
    state of the world through its actions is the agent itself.

-   **Determinism: Deterministic.** The outcome of every action is
    uniquely determined by the state in which it is performed. Moving
    forward into square \[x,y\] always results in being in \[x,y\] if
    it's empty, hitting a wall if it's a boundary, or dying if it
    contains a pit or the Wumpus (unless killed).

-   **Episodic/Sequential: Sequential.** The agent's task involves a
    sequence of actions. The choice of action at each step depends on
    past actions and percepts, and significantly impacts future
    possibilities and the eventual outcome. A single wrong move can lead
    to failure.

-   **Static/Dynamic: Static.** The locations of pits, the Wumpus (while
    alive), and the gold do not change while the agent is deliberating
    or performing an action. The environment only changes in response to
    the agent's actions (e.g., Wumpus becoming dead, agent changing
    location).

-   **Discrete/Continuous: Discrete.** The environment is a grid of
    discrete locations (4x4). Time proceeds in discrete steps (turns).
    The agent's percepts and actions are also discrete.

-   **Known/Unknown:** The *rules* of the environment (the \"physics\"
    -- how breezes relate to pits, stenches to the Wumpus, effects of
    actions) are known to the agent. However, the initial
    *configuration* of the environment -- specifically, the locations of
    the pits and the Wumpus (and the gold) -- is unknown.

    -   From one viewpoint, the transition model is fully known (e.g.,
        the agent knows that entering a pit square leads to death), but
        the current state description is incomplete.

    -   Alternatively, one could argue the transition model is
        effectively unknown initially, as the agent doesn't know the
        outcome of moving into an adjacent square (safe or death). This
        distinction is subtle and doesn't significantly alter the
        approach needed. The key point is the presence of critical
        unknown information.

### Need for Reasoning beyond Simple Exploration

The partial observability and the potentially lethal nature of the
Wumpus World make simple exploration strategies (like random movement or
uninformed search on the grid locations) extremely risky. An agent that
blindly enters an adjacent square might fall into a pit or encounter the
Wumpus.

To survive and succeed, the agent must use **logical reasoning**. It
needs to:

1.  Combine its knowledge of the Wumpus World rules with the percepts
    received.

2.  Infer properties of unobserved squares, such as whether they are
    safe or likely contain a hazard.

3.  Make decisions (which square to explore next, whether to shoot)
    based on these inferences.

This motivates the use of formal logic as the mechanism for representing
knowledge and performing inference within the agent.

# Logic Fundamentals {#sec:logic_fundamentals}

To build agents capable of reasoning, we need a formal system for
representing knowledge and deriving conclusions. Logic provides the
necessary mathematical foundation.

## Formal Languages: Representation and Derivation {#sec:formal_languages}

Logics are formal languages designed for knowledge representation and
reasoning. For our purposes, a logic provides two key capabilities:

1.  **Representation**: A precise *syntax* defines how to construct
    sentences (formulas) that represent facts, rules, and beliefs about
    the world. These sentences form the agent's Knowledge Base (*KB*).

2.  **Derivation (Inference)**: A set of rules or procedures allows the
    agent to systematically derive new sentences (conclusions) that are
    *logical consequences* of the sentences already present in the *KB*.
    This process allows the agent to make explicit knowledge that was
    only implicit in its *KB*.

::: examplebox
Example : Wumpus World Inference If an agent's *KB* contains the rule
\"A square is breezy if and only if an adjacent square contains a pit\"
and the fact \"Square \[1,1\] is not breezy\", the agent can use logical
inference to derive the conclusions \"Square \[1,2\] does not contain a
pit\" and \"Square \[2,1\] does not contain a pit\". Similarly, if the
*KB* contains \"Square \[1,1\] has no stench\", the agent can infer
\"Square \[1,2\] does not contain the Wumpus\" and \"Square \[2,1\] does
not contain the Wumpus\".
:::

## Syntax and Semantics {#sec:syntax_semantics}

Every logic is characterized by its syntax and semantics, which are
distinct but related concepts.

### Syntax: The Structure of Sentences

The **syntax** of a logic defines the rules for constructing valid
sentences, often called Well-Formed Formulas (WFFs). It specifies:

-   The set of allowed symbols (e.g., propositional symbols like $P, Q$,
    connectives like $\land, \lor, \neg$, quantifiers like
    $\forall, \exists$).

-   Grammatical rules for combining these symbols into complex
    sentences.

Syntax determines whether a string of symbols constitutes a legitimate
sentence in the language, irrespective of its meaning. For example, in
propositional logic, $P \land \neg Q$ might be syntactically correct,
while $P \neg \land Q$ would be incorrect.

### Semantics: The Meaning and Truth of Sentences

The **semantics** of a logic defines the meaning of sentences by
connecting them to possible states of the world. It provides a way to
determine the truth value (`True` or `False`) of any sentence.

-   **Models (Possible Worlds):** Semantics is often defined in terms of
    *models*. A model is a formal abstraction of a possible state of
    affairs or a \"possible world\". For instance, in the Wumpus World,
    each possible configuration of pits and Wumpus location corresponds
    to a different model.

-   **Interpretation:** Semantics specifies how to interpret the symbols
    of the language within a given model.

-   **Truth Evaluation:** Based on the interpretation, semantics defines
    rules for evaluating the truth value of any sentence within a
    specific model. A sentence $\alpha$ might be true in model $m_1$ but
    false in model $m_2$.

::: examplebox
Example : Semantics in Wumpus World Let $W_{\{,1},3\}$ be a sentence
meaning \"The Wumpus is in square \[1,3\]\".

-   In a model $m_1$ where the Wumpus is actually at \[1,3\], the
    sentence $\Wumpus{1,3}$ is `True`.

-   In a model $m_2$ where the Wumpus is at \[2,2\], the sentence
    $\Wumpus{1,3}$ is `False`.

-   If the agent shoots into \[1,3\] in model $m_1$ and kills the
    Wumpus, this transitions the world to a new model $m_3$. In $m_3$,
    the sentence $WumpusAlive^{}$ (meaning \"The Wumpus is alive\")
    would become `False`, even though it was `True` in $m_1$.
:::

::: tcolorbox
Think of natural language.

-   **Syntax** is like grammar. \"Colorless green ideas sleep
    furiously\" is syntactically correct English, even if nonsensical.
    \"Sleep ideas colorless furiously green\" is syntactically
    incorrect.

-   **Semantics** deals with meaning and truth. \"The Eiffel Tower is in
    Paris\" is syntactically correct and semantically true (in the real
    world model). \"The Eiffel Tower is in Rome\" is syntactically
    correct but semantically false.

Syntax determines if a sentence is well-formed; semantics determines if
it is true in a given context (model).
:::

## Entailment (Logical Consequence) {#sec:entailment}

A central concept in logic is entailment, which captures the idea of one
sentence necessarily following from others.

::: definitionbox
Definition : Entailment ($\models$) A set of sentences $\textit{KB}{}$
**entails** a sentence $\beta$ (written $\textit{KB}{} \models \beta$)
if and only if $\beta$ is true in every model in which all sentences in
$\textit{KB}{}$ are true. Equivalently, if $\alpha$ is a single
sentence, $\alpha \models \beta$ if $\beta$ is true in every model where
$\alpha$ is true.
$$\textit{KB}{} \models \beta \iff \text{Models}(\textit{KB}{}) \subseteq \text{Models}(\beta)$$
where $\text{Models}(\gamma)$ denotes the set of all models in which the
sentence(s) $\gamma$ are true.
:::

If $\textit{KB}{} \models \beta$, we say $\beta$ is a **logical
consequence** of $\textit{KB}{}$. Entailment is about the relationship
between sentences based purely on their meaning (semantics), regardless
of any specific inference procedure.

### Entailment Example in Wumpus World (via Model Checking) {#sec:entailment_example}

Let's revisit the scenario where the agent has visited \[1,1\] (finding
it safe: $\neg P_{1,1,,} \neg \Wumpus{1,1}$) and moved to \[2,1\], where
it perceives a breeze.

-   **Knowledge Base (*KB*) includes:**

    -   $\neg \Pit{1,1}$ (From initial state or prior visit)

    -   $\BreezeProp{2,1}$ (Inferred from percept at \[2,1\])

    -   $B_{2,1,\iff }(P_{1,1,\lor }P_{2,2,\lor }P_{3,1,)}$ (Rule for
        breeze at \[2,1\])

    -   $\neg \BreezeProp{1,1}$ (Inferred from percept at \[1,1\])

    -   $B_{1,1,\iff }(P_{1,2,\lor }P_{2,1,)}$ (Rule for breeze at
        \[1,1\])

-   **Relevant Inferences from *KB*:**

    -   From $\neg \BreezeProp{1,1}$ and its rule, we infer
        $\neg (P_{1,2,\lor }P_{2,1,)}$, which implies
        $\neg P_{1,2,\land }\neg \Pit{2,1}$. So, $\neg \Pit{1,2}$ and
        $\neg \Pit{2,1}$ are known.

    -   From $\BreezeProp{2,1}$ and its rule, we infer
        $P_{1,1,\lor }P_{2,2,\lor }\Pit{3,1}$.

    -   Combining this with $\neg \Pit{1,1}$, we conclude
        $P_{2,2,\lor }\Pit{3,1}$. There must be a pit in \[2,2\] or
        \[3,1\] (or both).

-   **Possible Models (Focusing on Pits in \[2,2\], \[3,1\]):**
    Considering only the locations relevant to the breeze in \[2,1\] and
    consistent with $\textit{KB}{}$, the possible pit configurations
    are:

    1.  $m_1$: Pit in \[2,2\] only ($P_{2,2,\land }\neg \Pit{3,1}$)

    2.  $m_2$: Pit in \[3,1\] only ($\neg P_{2,2,\land }\Pit{3,1}$)

    3.  $m_3$: Pits in both \[2,2\] and \[3,1\]
        ($P_{2,2,\land }\Pit{3,1}$)

    So, $\text{Models}(\textit{KB}{}) = \{m_1, m_2, m_3\}$ (restricted
    to these propositions).

Now, let's check entailment for specific queries using these models:

-   **Query 1: Is $\neg \Pit{1,2}$ entailed?
    ($\textit{KB}{} \models \neg \Pit{1,2}$?)** We already inferred
    $\neg \Pit{1,2}$ directly from the absence of breeze in \[1,1\].
    Let's verify using models. The sentence $\neg \Pit{1,2}$ is true in
    all models where there is no pit in \[1,2\]. Since our simplified
    models $m_1, m_2, m_3$ don't constrain $\Pit{1,2}$, we need to
    consider the full *KB*. As shown in the inference above,
    $\neg \Pit{1,2}$ is derivable, meaning it must hold in all models of
    the *KB*. Thus, $\textit{KB}{} \models \neg \Pit{1,2}$. The agent
    can safely conclude \[1,2\] has no pit.

-   **Query 2: Is $\neg \Pit{2,2}$ entailed?
    ($\textit{KB}{} \models \neg \Pit{2,2}$?)** We examine the truth of
    $\neg \Pit{2,2}$ in the models consistent with $\textit{KB}{}$:

    -   In $m_1$: $\Pit{2,2}$ is true, so $\neg \Pit{2,2}$ is `False`.

    -   In $m_2$: $\neg \Pit{2,2}$ is true.

    -   In $m_3$: $\Pit{2,2}$ is true, so $\neg \Pit{2,2}$ is `False`.

    Since $\neg \Pit{2,2}$ is not true in all models of $\textit{KB}{}$
    (it's false in $m_1$ and $m_3$), the entailment does not hold:
    $\textit{KB}{} \not\models \neg \Pit{2,2}$. The agent cannot
    conclude that \[2,2\] is safe.

-   **Query 3: Is $P_{2,2,\lor }\Pit{3,1}$ entailed?
    ($\textit{KB}{} \models P_{2,2,\lor }\Pit{3,1}$?)** We check the
    truth of $P_{2,2,\lor }\Pit{3,1}$ in the models:

    -   In $m_1$: $\Pit{2,2}$ is true, so the disjunction is `True`.

    -   In $m_2$: $\Pit{3,1}$ is true, so the disjunction is `True`.

    -   In $m_3$: Both $\Pit{2,2}$ and $\Pit{3,1}$ are true, so the
        disjunction is `True`.

    Since $P_{2,2,\lor }\Pit{3,1}$ is true in all models of
    $\textit{KB}{}$, the entailment holds:
    $\textit{KB}{} \models P_{2,2,\lor }\Pit{3,1}$.

This process of determining entailment by enumerating all relevant
models and checking the truth of the query sentence in each is called
**model checking**.

## Logical Inference: Finding Entailed Sentences {#sec:logical_inference}

While entailment defines what logically follows, model checking provides
a direct way to compute it. However, it's not always practical. We need
effective procedures, called *inference algorithms*, to find or derive
entailed sentences.

### Model Checking Approach (Semantic Inference)

This approach directly implements the definition of entailment by
exploring the space of possible models.

-   **Process:** Enumerate models consistent with the *KB*. Check if the
    query sentence $\beta$ holds in all of them.

-   **Pros:** Conceptually straightforward. Can be efficient for some
    types of problems, especially in propositional logic where the
    number of models is finite (though potentially large, $2^n$ for $n$
    symbols). Forms the basis of algorithms like DPLL for SAT solving.

-   **Cons:** Becomes computationally intractable as the number of
    propositional symbols grows. Infeasible for logics with infinite
    models (like first-order logic with numbers).

### Theorem Proving Approach (Syntactic Inference)

This approach uses *inference rules* to manipulate sentences
syntactically, deriving new sentences from existing ones without
explicitly constructing models.

-   **Inference Rules:** Syntactic patterns that license the derivation
    of a conclusion sentence from premise sentences. Examples include
    Modus Ponens, And Elimination, and Resolution (discussed later).

-   **Derivation/Proof:** A sequence of sentences, starting with the
    premises in the *KB*, where each subsequent sentence is derived from
    preceding sentences using an inference rule.

-   **Notation:** $\textit{KB}{} \vdash_i \beta$ means sentence $\beta$
    can be derived (proved) from $\textit{KB}{}$ using a specific set of
    inference rules $i$.

-   **Pros:** Can be more efficient than model checking, especially when
    the proof is short or the structure of the sentences guides the
    search. Applicable even when the number of models is infinite. Forms
    the basis of automated theorem provers and logic programming.

-   **Cons:** Finding a proof can be computationally hard. The search
    space of possible derivations can be very large. Requires careful
    design of inference rules and search strategies.

### Soundness and Completeness of Inference Algorithms

An inference procedure $\vdash$ should ideally relate correctly to the
semantic notion of entailment $\models$. Two key properties define this
relationship:

::: definitionbox
Definition : Soundness An inference algorithm $\vdash$ is **sound** if
it only derives sentences that are actually entailed.
$$\text{If } \textit{KB}{} \vdash \beta, \text{ then } \textit{KB}{} \models \beta$$
A sound algorithm never asserts a conclusion that is not a logical
consequence of the premises. It preserves truth. Soundness ensures that
the inference process does not invent conclusions that are not justified
by the knowledge base.
:::

::: definitionbox
Definition : Completeness An inference algorithm $\vdash$ is
**complete** if it can derive every sentence that is entailed.
$$\text{If } \textit{KB}{} \models \beta, \text{ then } \textit{KB}{} \vdash \beta$$
A complete algorithm guarantees that if a conclusion logically follows
from the premises, the algorithm is capable of finding a derivation for
it (given sufficient resources). Completeness ensures that the inference
process is powerful enough to find all logical consequences.
:::

::: mdframed
**Goal:** We seek inference algorithms that are both **sound** (don't
derive falsehoods) and **complete** (can derive all truths). Soundness
is typically essential, while completeness is highly desirable.
:::

## Overview of Logic Types {#sec:logic_types}

Not all logics are created equal; they differ in their expressive power
and computational complexity.

### Propositional Logic (PL)

-   **Basic Unit:** Propositions (atomic sentences) representing facts
    that are either true or false (e.g., $P$, $Q$, $Raining$,
    $\Pit{1,2}$).

-   **Syntax:** Combines atomic propositions using logical connectives
    ($\neg, \land, \lor, \implies, \iff$).

-   **Semantics:** Defined by truth assignments to atomic propositions
    and truth tables for connectives.

-   **Expressiveness:** Limited. Treats propositions as indivisible
    units. Cannot easily express properties of objects, relationships
    between them, or make generalized statements (e.g., \"All squares
    adjacent to a pit are breezy\").

-   **Complexity:** Deciding entailment is decidable but co-NP-complete
    in general (though often feasible in practice for many problems).

### First-Order Logic (FOL) (Predicate Calculus)

-   **Basic Units:** Objects, properties of objects (predicates),
    relations between objects, functions on objects.

-   **Syntax:** Includes terms (representing objects, e.g., constants,
    variables, function applications), atomic sentences (predicates
    applied to terms, e.g., $Brother(Richard, John)$,
    $At(Agent, [1,1])$), connectives (like PL), and quantifiers
    ($\forall$ - for all, $\exists$ - there exists) to generalize over
    objects.

-   **Semantics:** More complex. Requires defining a domain of objects
    and interpretations (mappings) for constants, predicates, and
    functions within a model.

-   **Expressiveness:** Significantly greater than PL. Allows
    representing complex relationships and making universal or
    existential statements about potentially infinite domains (e.g.,
    $\forall x, y \: Adjacent(x, y) \land Pit(x) \implies Breeze(y)$).

-   **Complexity:** Deciding entailment is undecidable in general (no
    algorithm exists that can always determine entailment in finite
    time), but it is semi-decidable (algorithms exist that can find a
    proof if one exists, but may run forever if no proof exists).

::: remarkbox
Note : This course will first focus on **Propositional Logic** due to
its simpler structure, allowing us to understand the core concepts of
logical representation and inference. We will explore its capabilities
and limitations (like the Frame Problem) before moving on to the more
expressive **First-Order Logic** in subsequent lectures.
:::

# Propositional Logic: Syntax and Semantics {#sec:pl_syntax_semantics}

We now formally define Propositional Logic (PL), the simplest logic we
will study, focusing on its structure (syntax) and meaning (semantics).

## Syntax {#sec:pl_syntax}

The syntax defines what constitutes a valid sentence in PL.

### Atomic Sentences (Propositional Symbols)

The fundamental building blocks are **atomic sentences** or
**propositional symbols**.

-   Each symbol represents a statement that can be definitively `True`
    or `False`.

-   They are indivisible units within the logic.

-   Symbols can be represented by uppercase letters (e.g., $P, Q, R$) or
    more descriptive names, often using subscripts for clarity (e.g.,
    $\Wumpus{1,3}$ for \"The Wumpus is in square \[1,3\]\",
    $\BreezeProp{1,1}$ for \"Square \[1,1\] is breezy\"). Using
    meaningful names improves readability for humans.

-   The two constant symbols $\texttt{True}$ (representing the
    proposition that is always true) and $\texttt{False}$ (representing
    the proposition that is always false) are also considered atomic
    sentences.

### Logical Connectives

Complex sentences are constructed by combining simpler sentences using
five standard **logical connectives**:

-   $\neg$ : **Negation** (NOT). $\neg P$ (\"not P\") is true if and
    only if $P$ is false.

-   $\land$ : **Conjunction** (AND). $P \land Q$ (\"P and Q\") is true
    if and only if both $P$ and $Q$ are true.

-   $\lor$ : **Disjunction** (OR). $P \lor Q$ (\"P or Q\") is true if
    and only if at least one of $P$ or $Q$ is true. This is an
    *inclusive* OR.

-   $\implies$ : **Implication** (Conditional, IF\...THEN\...).
    $P \implies Q$ (\"P implies Q\" or \"if P then Q\") is true unless
    $P$ is true and $Q$ is false. $P$ is called the antecedent (or
    premise), and $Q$ is the consequent (or conclusion).

-   $\iff$ : **Biconditional** (Equivalence, IF AND ONLY IF). $P \iff Q$
    (\"P if and only if Q\") is true if and only if $P$ and $Q$ have the
    same truth value (both true or both false).

### Formal Grammar (BNF)

The set of all well-formed sentences in PL can be defined recursively
using Backus-Naur Form (BNF):
$$\begin{aligned}    \textbf{Sentence} &\to \textbf{AtomicSentence} \mid \textbf{ComplexSentence} \\    \textbf{AtomicSentence} &\to \texttt{True}\mid \texttt{False}\mid P \mid Q \mid R \mid \dots \mid P_{1,1,\mid }\dots \\    \textbf{ComplexSentence} &\to (\textbf{Sentence}) && \text{Parenthesized sentence} \\    &\mid \neg \textbf{Sentence} && \text{Negation} \\    &\mid \textbf{Sentence} \land \textbf{Sentence} && \text{Conjunction} \\    &\mid \textbf{Sentence} \lor \textbf{Sentence} && \text{Disjunction} \\    &\mid \textbf{Sentence} \implies \textbf{Sentence} && \text{Implication} \\    &\mid \textbf{Sentence} \iff \textbf{Sentence} && \text{Biconditional}\end{aligned}$$
This grammar ensures that sentences are constructed unambiguously.
Parentheses are crucial for defining the scope of connectives.

### Operator Precedence

To minimize the use of parentheses and improve readability, a standard
order of precedence is defined for the connectives:

1.  $\neg$ (highest precedence)

2.  $\land$

3.  $\lor$

4.  $\implies$

5.  $\iff$ (lowest precedence)

Connectives are typically assumed to be left-associative when precedence
is equal (e.g., $A \land B \land C$ means $(A \land B) \land C$).

::: examplebox
Example : The sentence $\neg P \land Q \lor R \implies S$ is parsed
according to precedence as $(((\neg P) \land Q) \lor R) \implies S$.
:::

## Semantics {#sec:pl_semantics}

Semantics defines the meaning of PL sentences by specifying how to
determine their truth value.

### Truth Assignments (Models)

The core idea is to assign a truth value (`True` or `False`) to each
atomic propositional symbol.

::: definitionbox
Definition : Model in PL A **model** $m$ in propositional logic is a
complete assignment of truth values to all propositional symbols in the
language. If a language uses symbols $P_1, P_2, \dots, P_n$, a model
specifies a value (${\texttt{True}}$ or ${\texttt{False}}$) for each
$P_i$. A model represents one possible way the world could be,
concerning the truth of the basic propositions.
:::

::: examplebox
Example : If the only symbols are $P, Q, R$, then
$m = \{P=\texttt{True}, Q=\texttt{False}, R=\texttt{True}\}$ is one
possible model. There are $2^3 = 8$ possible models in this case. In
general, for $n$ propositional symbols, there are $2^n$ distinct models.
:::

### Truth Tables for Connectives

Once truth values are assigned to atomic sentences by a model, the truth
value of any complex sentence can be determined recursively using the
standard definitions of the connectives, often summarized in truth
tables:

::: center
     $P$       $Q$     $\neg P$   $P \land Q$   $P \lor Q$   $P \implies Q$   $P \iff Q$
  --------- --------- ---------- ------------- ------------ ---------------- ------------
   `False`   `False`    `True`      `False`      `False`         `True`         `True`
   `False`   `True`     `True`      `False`       `True`         `True`        `False`
   `True`    `False`   `False`      `False`       `True`        `False`        `False`
   `True`    `True`    `False`      `True`        `True`         `True`         `True`

:::

### Semantics of Implication (Material Implication)

The definition of $P \implies Q$ warrants special attention. It is known
as **material implication**.

-   It is defined to be equivalent to $\neg P \lor Q$.

-   It is only `False` when the antecedent ($P$) is `True` and the
    consequent ($Q$) is `False`.

-   In all other cases, it is `True`. This includes cases where the
    antecedent is false ($P$ is `False`), regardless of the truth value
    of the consequent ($Q$). This property is sometimes summarized as
    *\"ex falso quodlibet\"* (from falsehood, anything follows).

-   It's crucial to distinguish material implication from causation or
    relevance in natural language. $P \implies Q$ does not mean $P$
    causes $Q$, only that it's not the case that $P$ is true while $Q$
    is false.

::: examplebox
Example : The sentence \"If the moon is made of green cheese, then
$2+2=5$\" is considered `True` under material implication, because the
antecedent (\"the moon is made of green cheese\") is false.
:::

### Relation between Implication and Entailment (Deduction Theorem)

There's a fundamental link between the syntactic connective $\implies$
and the semantic notion of entailment $\models$.

::: theorembox
Theorem : Deduction Theorem for PL For any sentences $\alpha$ and
$\beta$, $\alpha \models \beta$ if and only if the sentence
$(\alpha \implies \beta)$ is **valid** (i.e., true in all possible
models). This theorem connects the semantic concept of entailment (truth
preservation across all models) with the syntactic concept of
implication and the semantic property of validity (truth in all models).
It provides a method to check entailment by checking the validity of a
single implication sentence.
:::

This theorem provides a bridge between checking entailment (a semantic
task involving potentially many models) and proving the validity of a
single implication sentence (which can sometimes be done syntactically).
It highlights that entailment captures the essence of logical
implication: if $\alpha$ entails $\beta$, then the truth of $\alpha$
guarantees the truth of $\beta$.

### Satisfiability, Validity, and Unsatisfiability

Based on the evaluation of sentences in models, we can define important
semantic properties:

-   **Satisfiability:** A sentence $\alpha$ is *satisfiable* if it is
    true in at least one model.

-   **Validity (Tautology):** A sentence $\alpha$ is *valid* (or a
    *tautology*) if it is true in all possible models (e.g.,
    $P \lor \neg P$). Valid sentences are necessarily true based on
    their logical structure alone.

-   **Unsatisfiability (Contradiction):** A sentence $\alpha$ is
    *unsatisfiable* (or a *contradiction*) if it is false in all
    possible models (e.g., $P \land \neg P$).

These concepts are related to entailment:

-   $\textit{KB}{} \models \beta$ if and only if
    $(\textit{KB}{} \implies \beta)$ is valid (Deduction Theorem, where
    $\textit{KB}{}$ is treated as the conjunction of its sentences).

-   $\textit{KB}{} \models \beta$ if and only if
    $(\textit{KB}{} \land \neg \beta)$ is unsatisfiable. This forms the
    basis for *proof by contradiction* or *refutation* methods in
    inference.

# Inference in Propositional Logic {#sec:inference_pl}

Having defined the syntax and semantics of propositional logic, we now
turn to **inference**: the process of deriving new sentences
(conclusions) from existing ones (premises in the *KB*). We focus
primarily on the syntactic approach, known as theorem proving, which
relies on the application of inference rules.

## Inference as Search {#sec:inference_search}

The task of determining whether $\textit{KB}{} \models \beta$ (or
finding $\beta$ such that $\textit{KB}{} \models \beta$) can be framed
as a search problem.

-   **States:** Represent the set of sentences currently known or
    derived. The initial state contains only the sentences in the
    original *KB*.

-   **Actions/Operators:** Applications of sound inference rules.
    Applying a rule to sentences in the current state generates a new
    sentence, leading to a successor state that includes the new
    sentence.

-   **Goal State:** A state that contains the target sentence $\beta$
    (if we are trying to prove a specific $\beta$), or a state where no
    new sentences can be derived (if we are trying to find all
    consequences).

-   **Search Strategies:** Standard search algorithms can be adapted.

    -   **Forward Chaining (Data-Driven):** Start with the *KB* and
        repeatedly apply inference rules to derive all possible
        conclusions until the goal sentence is found or no more
        inferences can be made.

    -   **Backward Chaining (Goal-Driven):** Start with the goal
        sentence $\beta$ and work backward. To prove $\beta$, find rules
        whose conclusion is $\beta$ and try to prove their premises.
        This is often more focused if the goal is known.

::: remarkbox
Remark : It is crucial to understand that this search occurs in the
space of logical derivations, manipulating sentences based on inference
rules. It is distinct from searching the state space of the agent's
environment (e.g., the grid locations in the Wumpus World). We are
searching for a *proof*, not a physical path for the agent.
:::

## Inference Rules {#sec:inference_rules}

Inference rules are patterns that specify how to derive valid
conclusions from a set of premises. They are designed to be *sound*,
meaning they only generate conclusions that are entailed by the
premises.

::: mdframed
An inference rule is typically written as:
$$\frac{\text{Premise}_1, \quad \text{Premise}_2, \quad \dots}{\text{Conclusion}}$$
This means that if all the premises above the line are known to be true,
then the conclusion below the line can be validly inferred.
:::

Here are some fundamental inference rules for propositional logic:

### Modus Ponens (MP)

Latin for \"mode that affirms\". This is perhaps the most famous
inference rule.
$$\frac{\alpha, \quad \alpha \implies \beta}{\beta} \quad (\text{Modus Ponens})$$
If we know an implication ($\alpha \implies \beta$) holds and its
antecedent ($\alpha$) is true, we can infer that the consequent
($\beta$) is true. This rule is central to forward chaining.

### Modus Tollens (MT)

Latin for \"mode that denies\". It works with the contrapositive form of
implication.
$$\frac{\alpha \implies \beta, \quad \neg \beta}{\neg \alpha} \quad (\text{Modus Tollens})$$
If we know an implication holds and its consequent ($\beta$) is false,
we can infer that the antecedent ($\alpha$) must be false.

### And Elimination ($\land$ E)

Also known as Simplification. From a conjunction, we can infer any of
its individual conjuncts.
$$\frac{\alpha_1 \land \alpha_2 \land \dots \land \alpha_n}{\alpha_i} \quad (\text{And Elimination})$$
For example, from $P \land Q$, we can infer $P$. We can also infer $Q$.

### And Introduction ($\land$ I)

Also known as Conjunction. If we know several sentences are true
individually, we can infer their conjunction.
$$\frac{\alpha_1, \quad \alpha_2, \quad \dots, \quad \alpha_n}{\alpha_1 \land \alpha_2 \land \dots \land \alpha_n} \quad (\text{And Introduction})$$
For example, if we know $P$ and we know $Q$, we can infer $P \land Q$.

### Or Introduction ($\lor$ I)

Also known as Addition. If we know a sentence is true, we can infer its
disjunction with any other sentence.
$$\frac{\alpha_i}{\alpha_1 \lor \alpha_2 \lor \dots \lor \alpha_n} \quad (\text{Or Introduction})$$
For example, if we know $P$, we can infer $P \lor Q$ (for any $Q$).

### Resolution

Resolution is a single, powerful inference rule that is sound and,
importantly, *refutation-complete* for propositional logic when
sentences are converted into a specific format called Conjunctive Normal
Form (CNF).

#### Preliminaries: Literals and Clauses

-   A **literal** is an atomic sentence (a positive literal, e.g., $P$)
    or the negation of an atomic sentence (a negative literal, e.g.,
    $\neg P$).

-   A **clause** is a disjunction of one or more literals (e.g.,
    $P \lor \neg Q \lor R$). A single literal is also considered a
    clause.

-   **Conjunctive Normal Form (CNF):** A sentence is in CNF if it is a
    conjunction of clauses. Example:
    $(P \lor \neg Q) \land (\neg P \lor R) \land Q$. Any propositional
    logic sentence can be converted into an equivalent CNF sentence
    (though the conversion might increase the sentence size). Resolution
    typically operates on sentences in CNF.

#### The Resolution Rule:

The rule works by identifying two clauses containing complementary
literals (e.g., $P$ in one clause and $\neg P$ in another) and producing
a new clause (the resolvent) containing all literals from both original
clauses except the complementary pair.

-   **Unit Resolution:** A special case where one clause is just a
    single literal (a unit clause).
    $$\frac{l_1 \lor \dots \lor l_k, \quad \neg l_i}{l_1 \lor \dots \lor l_{i-1} \lor l_{i+1} \lor \dots \lor l_k} \quad (\text{Unit Resolution})$$
    If we have a disjunction and know that one of the disjuncts ($l_i$)
    is false (i.e., we know $\neg l_i$), we can remove $l_i$ from the
    clause.

-   **Generalized Resolution Rule:** The general form operates on any
    two clauses.
    $$\frac{l_1 \lor \dots \lor l_i \lor \dots \lor l_k, \quad m_1 \lor \dots \lor \neg l_i \lor \dots \lor m_n}{(l_1 \lor \dots \lor l_{i-1} \lor l_{i+1} \lor \dots \lor l_k) \lor (m_1 \lor \dots \lor m_{j-1} \lor m_{j+1} \lor \dots \lor m_n)} \quad (\text{Resolution})$$
    where $l_i$ is a literal appearing in the first clause, and
    $\neg l_i$ is its complement appearing in the second clause. The
    resulting clause contains all literals from the original clauses
    except for the complementary pair $l_i$ and $\neg l_i$. If the
    resolvent contains both $L$ and $\neg L$ for some literal $L$, it is
    a tautology and can be discarded. If the resolvent contains
    duplicate literals, they can be merged (e.g.,
    $A \lor B \lor A \equiv A \lor B$). If resolution produces an empty
    clause (containing no literals), this represents $\texttt{False}$
    and signifies a contradiction in the original set of clauses.

::: remarkbox
Intuition behind Resolution Consider the premises $A \lor X$ and
$B \lor \neg X$. We want to show they entail $A \lor B$. We can reason
by cases based on the truth value of $X$:

-   **Case 1: Assume $X$ is True.** For the second premise
    $B \lor \neg X$ to be true, since $\neg X$ is False, $B$ must be
    True. If $B$ is True, then $A \lor B$ must be True.

-   **Case 2: Assume $X$ is False.** For the first premise $A \lor X$ to
    be true, since $X$ is False, $A$ must be True. If $A$ is True, then
    $A \lor B$ must be True.

Since $X$ must be either True or False, and in both cases $A \lor B$
follows, we can conclude $A \lor B$. The resolution rule captures this
case analysis syntactically.
$$\frac{A \lor X, \quad B \lor \neg X}{A \lor B}$$ This also relates to
implication: $A \lor X \equiv \neg A \implies X$ and
$B \lor \neg X \equiv X \implies B$. Resolution acts like a generalized
form of transitivity: from $\neg A \implies X$ and $X \implies B$, we
get $\neg A \implies B$, which is equivalent to $A \lor B$.
:::

::: examplebox
Resolution Example in Wumpus World Recall the scenario: Agent in \[2,1\]
perceives breeze. Knows $\neg \Pit{1,1}$. Rule:
$B_{2,1,\iff }(P_{1,1,\lor }P_{2,2,\lor }P_{3,1,)}$.

1.  **Convert knowledge to CNF clauses:**

    -   From $\BreezeProp{2,1}$ and the rule (using biconditional
        elimination, implication elimination):
        $P_{1,1,\lor }P_{2,2,\lor }\Pit{3,1}$ (Clause $C_1$)

    -   From known fact: $\neg \Pit{1,1}$ (Clause $C_2$)

2.  **Apply Resolution:** Resolve $C_1$ and $C_2$ on the complementary
    literals $\Pit{1,1}$ and $\neg \Pit{1,1}$.
    $$\frac{P_{1,1,\lor }P_{2,2,\lor }P_{3,1,,} \quad \neg \Pit{1,1}}{P_{2,2,\lor }\Pit{3,1}}$$
    Resulting clause $C_3: P_{2,2,\lor }\Pit{3,1}$. This means there
    must be a pit in \[2,2\] or \[3,1\].

3.  **Incorporate new knowledge:** Suppose the agent later visits
    \[1,2\] and perceives no breeze. This implies
    $\neg \BreezeProp{1,2}$. The rule is
    $B_{1,2,\iff }(P_{1,1,\lor }P_{2,2,\lor }P_{1,3,)}$.

    -   From $\neg \BreezeProp{1,2}$ and the rule:
        $\neg (P_{1,1,\lor }P_{2,2,\lor }P_{1,3,)}$

    -   Applying De Morgan's Law:
        $\neg P_{1,1,\land }\neg P_{2,2,\land }\neg \Pit{1,3}$

    -   Using And Elimination, we get three unit clauses, including:
        $\neg \Pit{2,2}$ (Clause $C_4$)

4.  **Apply Resolution again:** Resolve $C_3$ and $C_4$ on $\Pit{2,2}$
    and $\neg \Pit{2,2}$.
    $$\frac{P_{2,2,\lor }P_{3,1,,} \quad \neg \Pit{2,2}}{\Pit{3,1}}$$
    Resulting clause $C_5: \Pit{3,1}$. The agent has successfully
    inferred the exact location of a pit in \[3,1\].
:::

#### Refutation Completeness:

Resolution's key property is **refutation completeness**. This means:

-   If a set of clauses $S$ is unsatisfiable (contains a contradiction),
    then repeated application of the resolution rule is guaranteed to
    eventually derive the empty clause ($\texttt{False}$).

-   This allows us to prove entailment $\textit{KB}{} \models \alpha$
    using proof by contradiction:

    1.  Add the negation of the goal, $\neg \alpha$, to the knowledge
        base $\textit{KB}{}$.

    2.  Convert all sentences in $\textit{KB}{} \cup \{\neg \alpha\}$
        into CNF.

    3.  Apply the resolution rule repeatedly to the set of clauses.

    4.  If the empty clause is derived, the original set
        $\textit{KB}{} \cup \{\neg \alpha\}$ is unsatisfiable, which
        means $\textit{KB}{}$ must entail $\alpha$.

    5.  If the empty clause cannot be derived and no new clauses can be
        generated, then $\textit{KB}{}$ does not entail $\alpha$.

::: remarkbox
Remark : While resolution is complete, the process of deriving the empty
clause (or determining that it cannot be derived) can take time
exponential in the number of symbols in the worst case. However, many
heuristics and optimized implementations (like those used in modern SAT
solvers based on the DPLL algorithm, which incorporates resolution) make
it practical for many problems.
:::

## Common Logical Equivalences {#sec:logical_equivalences}

Logical equivalences are pairs of sentences that have the same truth
value in all models ($\alpha \equiv \beta$ means $\alpha \models \beta$
and $\beta \models \alpha$). They are not inference rules themselves,
but are crucial tools for rewriting sentences into simpler or standard
forms (like CNF) without changing their meaning. Applying equivalences
is often a preprocessing step before using inference rules like
resolution.

Key equivalences include:

-   **Commutativity:**
    $(\alpha \land \beta) \equiv (\beta \land \alpha)$;
    $(\alpha \lor \beta) \equiv (\beta \lor \alpha)$

-   **Associativity:**
    $((\alpha \land \beta) \land \gamma) \equiv (\alpha \land (\beta \land \gamma))$;
    $((\alpha \lor \beta) \lor \gamma) \equiv (\alpha \lor (\beta \lor \gamma))$

-   **Idempotence:** $(\alpha \land \alpha) \equiv \alpha$;
    $(\alpha \lor \alpha) \equiv \alpha$

-   **Double Negation Elimination:** $\neg (\neg \alpha) \equiv \alpha$

-   **Contraposition:**
    $(\alpha \implies \beta) \equiv (\neg \beta \implies \neg \alpha)$

-   **Implication Elimination:**
    $(\alpha \implies \beta) \equiv (\neg \alpha \lor \beta)$

-   **Biconditional Elimination:**
    $(\alpha \iff \beta) \equiv ((\alpha \implies \beta) \land (\beta \implies \alpha)) \equiv ((\neg \alpha \lor \beta) \land (\neg \beta \lor \alpha))$

-   **De Morgan's Laws:**
    $\neg (\alpha \land \beta) \equiv (\neg \alpha \lor \neg \beta)$;
    $\neg (\alpha \lor \beta) \equiv (\neg \alpha \land \neg \beta)$

-   **Distributivity:**
    $(\alpha \land (\beta \lor \gamma)) \equiv ((\alpha \land \beta) \lor (\alpha \land \gamma))$;
    $(\alpha \lor (\beta \land \gamma)) \equiv ((\alpha \lor \beta) \land (\alpha \lor \gamma))$

-   **Absorption Laws:**
    $(\alpha \land (\alpha \lor \beta)) \equiv \alpha$;
    $(\alpha \lor (\alpha \land \beta)) \equiv \alpha$

(And others involving $\texttt{True}$ and $\texttt{False}$). These
equivalences form the basis of algebraic manipulation of logical
sentences.

## Example Proof using Inference Rules and Equivalences {#sec:example_proof}

Let's formally derive $\neg \Pit{1,2}$ (Square \[1,2\] is safe from
pits) given the agent's initial knowledge in the Wumpus World,
demonstrating the application of various rules.

#### Knowledge Base (*KB*):

-   $R_1: \neg \Pit{1,1}$ (Game rule/Initial state)

-   $R_2: B_{1,1,\iff }(P_{1,2,\lor }P_{2,1,)}$ (Physics rule for
    \[1,1\])

-   $R_3: \neg \BreezeProp{1,1}$ (Percept at \[1,1\] at time 0)

#### Goal:

Prove $\neg \Pit{1,2}$.

::: proof
*Derivation using Inference Rules.* We start with the premises in the
*KB* and apply rules and equivalences step-by-step.

1.  $B_{1,1,\iff }(P_{1,2,\lor }P_{2,1,)}$ (Premise $R_2$)

2.  $(B_{1,1,\implies }(P_{1,2,\lor }P_{2,1,)}) \land ((P_{1,2,\lor }P_{2,1,)} \implies B_{1,1,)}$
    (Biconditional Elimination on 1)

3.  $(P_{1,2,\lor }P_{2,1,)} \implies \BreezeProp{1,1}$ (And Elimination
    ($\land$ E) on 2)

4.  $\neg B_{1,1,\implies }\neg (P_{1,2,\lor }P_{2,1,)}$ (Contraposition
    on 3)

5.  $\neg \BreezeProp{1,1}$ (Premise $R_3$)

6.  $\neg (P_{1,2,\lor }P_{2,1,)}$ (Modus Ponens (MP) on 4 and 5)

7.  $\neg P_{1,2,\land }\neg \Pit{2,1}$ (De Morgan's Law on 6)

8.  $\neg \Pit{1,2}$ (And Elimination ($\land$ E) on 7)

The derivation successfully concludes $\neg \Pit{1,2}$, demonstrating
that square \[1,2\] is provably safe based on the initial knowledge and
percepts. This sequence of steps constitutes a formal proof within the
system of propositional logic. ◻
:::

# A Propositional Logic Agent for the Wumpus World {#sec:pl_agent_wumpus}

We now apply the concepts of propositional logic to design a
knowledge-based agent capable of reasoning and acting in the Wumpus
World. This involves constructing a suitable Knowledge Base (*KB*) and
understanding how the agent uses inference. This process also reveals
significant challenges inherent in using propositional logic for dynamic
environments.

## Building the Knowledge Base (*KB*) {#sec:building_kb}

The *KB* must encode the agent's knowledge about the Wumpus World,
including static rules and dynamic information.

### Initial State and Static Rules (Atemporal Knowledge)

These are facts and rules that hold true throughout the agent's
existence in the environment.

-   **Initial State Facts:** The agent starts in \[1,1\], which is known
    to be safe.

    -   $\neg \Pit{1,1}$ (No pit in \[1,1\])

    -   $\neg \Wumpus{1,1}$ (No Wumpus in \[1,1\])

-   **Environment Physics (Percept Rules):** These rules define the
    relationship between hazards (pits, Wumpus) and percepts (breeze,
    stench). They must be specified for *every* square.

    -   For square \[1,1\]:

        -   $B_{1,1,\iff }(P_{1,2,\lor }P_{2,1,)}$ (Square \[1,1\] is
            breezy iff \[1,2\] or \[2,1\] has a pit)

        -   $S_{1,1,\iff }(W_{1,2,\lor }W_{2,1,)}$ (Square \[1,1\] is
            stinky iff \[1,2\] or \[2,1\] has the Wumpus)

    -   For square \[1,2\]:

        -   $B_{1,2,\iff }(P_{1,1,\lor }P_{2,2,\lor }P_{1,3,)}$

        -   $S_{1,2,\iff }(W_{1,1,\lor }W_{2,2,\lor }W_{1,3,)}$

    -   \... and so on for all 16 squares.

    Note that $\Pit{x,y}$, $\Wumpus{x,y}$, $\BreezeProp{x,y}$, and
    $\StenchProp{x,y}$ represent static properties of the squares
    themselves.

-   **Size Implication:** Writing these rules explicitly requires
    $16 \times 2 = 32$ biconditional sentences, each potentially
    expanding into multiple clauses in CNF. This is manageable but
    already hints at scalability issues.

### Representing Uniqueness Constraints

The rules state there is exactly one Wumpus. This needs to be encoded
explicitly.

Encoding such constraints, while possible, significantly increases the
size of the *KB*. Similar constraints might be needed for the gold,
though typically not for pits (which can be multiple).

## Handling Time and Change {#sec:handling_time}

The Wumpus World is dynamic from the agent's perspective: its location
changes, its percepts change, the Wumpus's status might change (alive to
dead), and its possession of the arrow changes. Representing this change
within propositional logic poses a challenge.

### The Problem with Static Symbols for Dynamic Properties

If we try to use static propositional symbols for properties that change
over time (like the agent's percepts or location), we run into
contradictions.

::: examplebox
Example : Contradiction with Static Percept Symbol Suppose we use a
single symbol $Stench$ to mean \"The agent currently perceives a
stench\".

-   Time $t=4$: Agent enters a square adjacent to the Wumpus and
    perceives a stench. We add $Stench$ to the *KB*.

-   Time $t=5$: Agent moves away and no longer perceives a stench. We
    add $\neg Stench$ to the *KB*.

-   **Problem:** The *KB* now contains both $Stench$ and $\neg Stench$.
    This is a logical contradiction ($P \land \neg P$). From a
    contradiction, any arbitrary sentence can be derived (via the
    principle of explosion, $P \land \neg P \models Q$), rendering the
    *KB* useless for sound reasoning.
:::

### Introducing Time Indices

The standard solution in propositional logic is to make propositions
time-dependent by adding a **time index** (often as a superscript) to
symbols representing properties that change.

-   $Stench^{4}$: The agent perceives a stench at time $t=4$.

-   $Breeze^{5}$: The agent perceives a breeze at time $t=5$.

-   $\neg Stench^{5}$: The agent does not perceive a stench at time
    $t=5$.

Now, $Stench^{4}$ and $\neg Stench^{5}$ are distinct propositional
symbols. They can coexist in the *KB* without causing a contradiction,
correctly reflecting the change in perception over time.

### Fluents: Time-Varying Propositions

Symbols representing properties that can change their truth value over
time are called **fluents**.

-   **Fluent Examples:**

    -   $L_{x,y}^{t}$: Agent is at location $(x,y)$ at time $t$. (e.g.,
        $L_{1,1}^{0}$)

    -   $FacingEast^{t}$: Agent is facing East at time $t$. (Similarly
        $\mathit{FacingNorth}^t$, etc.)

    -   $HaveArrow^{t}$: Agent possesses the arrow at time $t$.

    -   $WumpusAlive^{t}$: The Wumpus is alive at time $t$.

    -   $Breeze^{t}$, $Stench^{t}$, $\mathit{Glitter}^t$,
        $\mathit{Bump}^t$, $\mathit{Scream}^t$: Percept symbols at time
        $t$.

-   **Atemporal (Non-Fluent) Symbols:** Represent static properties of
    the environment that do not change over time.

    -   $\Pit{x,y}$: There is a pit in square $(x,y)$.

    -   $\Wumpus{x,y}$: The Wumpus (initially) is in square $(x,y)$.
        (Note: $WumpusAlive^{t}$ is the fluent tracking if it's
        currently alive).

    -   $\BreezeProp{x,y}$, $\StenchProp{x,y}$: The intrinsic property
        of square $(x,y)$ being breezy or stinky (due to adjacent
        hazards).

This explicit representation of time via indices allows PL to handle
dynamic aspects, but at the cost of proliferating symbols for each time
step.

### Connecting Percepts to State across Time

The agent's percepts at a given time depend on its state (location) and
the static properties of the environment at that location. This
relationship must be encoded in the *KB*. Example: \"If the agent is at
(x,y) at time t, then it perceives a breeze at time t if and only if the
square (x,y) has the intrinsic breeze property.\"
$$\forall x,y,t: \quad L_{x,y}^{t} \implies (Breeze^{t} \iff B_{x,y,)}$$
Since PL cannot express quantification ($\forall$), this must be written
out as separate axioms for each square $(x,y)$ and potentially each
relevant time step $t$.
$$L_{1,1}^{t} \implies (Breeze^{t} \iff B_{1,1,)}$$
$$L_{1,2}^{t} \implies (Breeze^{t} \iff B_{1,2,)}$$ $$\dots$$ Similar
rules are needed for stench and glitter. This further increases the size
of the *KB*, especially as the time horizon grows.

## Representing Actions and State Transitions {#sec:representing_actions}

To reason about the future and plan sequences of actions, the agent
needs knowledge about how actions affect the state of the world (i.e.,
the truth values of fluents). This requires a **transition model**.

### Need for a Transition Model

The transition model describes how the world state at time $t+1$ depends
on the state at time $t$ and the action performed at time $t$. In PL
with time indices, this is typically encoded using **successor-state
axioms**.

### Successor-State Axioms

A successor-state axiom defines the truth value of a fluent at time
$t+1$ based on the state and action at time $t$.

::: examplebox
Example : Effect of Forward Action on Location Consider the fluent
$L_{2,1}^{t+1}$ (being at \[2,1\] at time $t+1$). When is this true?
It's true if the agent was at \[1,1\] facing East at time $t$ and moved
Forward, OR if it was at \[2,2\] facing South at $t$ and moved Forward,
OR \... A simplified axiom focusing only on one cause: \"If the agent is
at \[1,1\] at time $t$, facing East, and performs the $Forward$ action
at time $t$, then at time $t+1$ the agent will be at \[2,1\].\"
$$(L_{1,1}^{t} \land FacingEast^{t} \land Forward^{t}) \implies L_{2,1}^{t+1}$$
This captures the *positive effect* of the action. We also need to
capture *negative effects*: \"If the agent is at \[1,1\] at time $t$,
facing East, and performs the $Forward$ action at time $t$, then at time
$t+1$ the agent will *not* be at \[1,1\].\"
$$(L_{1,1}^{t} \land FacingEast^{t} \land Forward^{t}) \implies \neg L_{1,1}^{t+1}$$
:::

#### Completeness Requirement:

To fully specify the transition model, such axioms must cover:

-   All possible actions
    ($Forward, TurnRight, TurnLeft, Grab, Shoot, Climb$). We need
    symbols like $Forward^{t}, TurnRight^{t}, \dots$ to represent the
    action performed at time $t$.

-   All fluents that can be affected by actions.

-   All conditions under which a fluent's value changes.

-   Crucially, all conditions under which a fluent's value *does not*
    change (this leads to the Frame Problem, see
    [5.5](#sec:frame_problem){reference-type="ref+Label"
    reference="sec:frame_problem"}).

Writing a complete set of successor-state axioms in PL becomes extremely
verbose, requiring axioms for every fluent, every action, and every time
step considered.

## Reasoning with the Temporal KB {#sec:reasoning_temporal}

With the temporal *KB* constructed, the agent can perform inference over
time.

### Positive Inference Example (Inferring New Location)

The agent can use inference rules like Modus Ponens to predict the
outcome of its actions.

-   **Assume *KB* contains at time $t=0$:**

    -   $L_{1,1}^{0}$ (Fact)

    -   $FacingEast^{0}$ (Fact)

    -   $Forward^{0}$ (Agent decides to execute Forward action)

    -   $(L_{1,1}^{0} \land FacingEast^{0} \land Forward^{0}) \implies L_{2,1}^{1}$
        (Instance of successor-state axiom)

-   **Inference:** By applying Modus Ponens to these sentences, the
    agent can derive: $$L_{2,1}^{1}$$

The agent successfully deduces its location at the next time step,
$t=1$.

### The Challenge: Inferring What \*Doesn't\* Change

A significant difficulty arises when trying to infer that properties
*remain the same* after an action, if that action doesn't explicitly
affect them.

::: examplebox
Example : Inferring Arrow Possession

-   **Assume *KB* contains at time $t=0$:**

    -   $HaveArrow^{0}$ (Agent starts with the arrow)

    -   $Forward^{0}$ (Agent performs Forward action)

    -   Successor-state axioms for location (as above).

-   **Query:** Does the agent have the arrow at time $t=1$?
    ($\textit{KB}{} \models HaveArrow^{1}$?)

-   **Problem:** Based only on the axioms provided so far (which
    describe location changes), the answer is **no**. There is no axiom
    stating that the $Forward$ action preserves the arrow. The *KB* does
    not contain enough information to conclude $HaveArrow^{1}$.

-   **Worse:** The agent also cannot conclude $\neg HaveArrow^{1}$. It
    simply remains ignorant about the arrow's status at $t=1$.

This seems counter-intuitive; common sense suggests that moving forward
shouldn't affect whether the agent holds an arrow. However, logic
requires explicit statements.
:::

## Limitation: The Frame Problem {#sec:frame_problem}

The difficulty in representing and reasoning about properties that *do
not* change after an action is known as the **Frame Problem**.

:::: definitionbox
The Frame Problem

::: definitionbox
Definition : Frame Problem The **Frame Problem** is the challenge of
concisely and efficiently representing the *non-effects* of actions. In
a logical framework like PL with successor-state axioms, it manifests as
the need to explicitly state all the fluents that *remain unchanged* by
each action. Humans naturally assume **inertia**: things tend to stay
the same unless explicitly acted upon. Representing this assumption
efficiently in logic is difficult.
:::
::::

### Illustration (HasArrow Example)

To allow the agent to correctly infer $HaveArrow^{1}$ after the
$Forward^{0}$ action, the *KB* must include explicit **frame axioms**
that state the conditions under which the arrow status persists. Example
Frame Axiom for $\HaveArrow$: \"If the agent has the arrow at time $t$
and the action performed at time $t$ is not $Shoot$, then the agent
still has the arrow at time $t+1$.\"
$$(HaveArrow^{t} \land \neg Shoot^{t}) \implies HaveArrow^{t+1}$$ (A
more complete axiom would list all actions that don't affect the arrow).

### Implications for KB Size and Representational Complexity

-   **Axiom Proliferation:** Frame axioms must be specified for nearly
    every combination of fluent and action that does *not* cause a
    change. If there are $F$ fluents and $A$ actions, the number of
    frame axioms can be roughly proportional to $F \times A$.

-   **KB Size:** In the Wumpus World, with potentially dozens of
    location fluents, orientation fluents, $\HaveArrow$, $\WumpusAlive$,
    etc., and several actions, the number of frame axioms becomes
    enormous, dwarfing the number of effect axioms.

-   **Manageability:** Constructing and maintaining such a large *KB* is
    tedious and error-prone.

-   **Inference Cost:** Inference algorithms become significantly slower
    when operating on a vastly larger set of axioms.

The Frame Problem was a major obstacle identified in early AI research,
highlighting a fundamental limitation of using simple logical
representations for dynamic worlds. It suggested that purely deductive
reasoning based on explicitly stated effects and non-effects might not
scale effectively.

::: remarkbox
Note : While initially seen as a potentially fatal flaw for logic-based
AI, various solutions and alternative formalisms (e.g., non-monotonic
logics, situation calculus with default assumptions, STRIPS
representations) have been developed to address the Frame Problem more
elegantly than explicitly listing all frame axioms. However, it remains
a key conceptual challenge in knowledge representation and reasoning
about action and change.
:::

# Conclusion and Outlook {#sec:conclusion}

This lecture introduced propositional logic as a formal framework for
enabling knowledge representation and reasoning in intelligent agents.
We explored its fundamental components and applied them to the Wumpus
World scenario.

## Summary: Propositional Logic for Knowledge-Based Agents

-   **Formal Representation:** Propositional Logic (PL) provides a
    well-defined syntax for expressing facts and rules as sentences, and
    clear semantics based on truth assignments in models.

-   **Inference Capabilities:** Agents can employ logical inference
    procedures (either semantic model checking or syntactic theorem
    proving using rules like Modus Ponens and Resolution) to derive new
    knowledge (logical consequences) from their *KB*, going beyond
    explicitly stored information.

-   **Wumpus World KB Construction:** We outlined the process of
    building a *KB* for the Wumpus World agent, encoding:

    -   Static knowledge: Initial state facts, environment physics
        (percept rules), uniqueness constraints.

    -   Dynamic knowledge: Using time indices (superscripts) to create
        *fluents* for representing time-varying properties (location,
        percepts, agent status).

    -   Action effects: Using *successor-state axioms* to model how
        actions change the state (fluents) over time.

-   **Reasoning Over Time:** The temporal *KB* allows the agent to
    reason about the effects of its actions and make predictions about
    future states.

## Key Challenge: The Frame Problem

Despite its formal clarity, applying PL to dynamic environments like the
Wumpus World reveals a major challenge:

-   **Representing Non-Change:** PL requires explicit statements not
    only about what changes due to an action (effect axioms) but also
    about everything that *remains unchanged* (frame axioms).

-   **Axiom Explosion:** The need for numerous frame axioms leads to an
    explosion in the size and complexity of the *KB*, making
    representation cumbersome and inference potentially inefficient.

-   **Scalability Impact:** This **Frame Problem** significantly hinders
    the scalability and practical application of pure propositional
    logic for representing knowledge in complex, dynamic domains where
    most properties persist over time.

## Preview: Addressing Limitations and Moving to First-Order Logic

The limitations encountered, particularly the representational
inefficiency stemming from the atomic nature of propositions and the
Frame Problem, motivate the exploration of more powerful logical
frameworks.

-   **Addressing the Frame Problem:** While solutions exist within
    logical frameworks (e.g., assuming inertia by default, specialized
    action representations), the inherent verbosity of PL remains.

-   **Next Steps: First-Order Logic (FOL):** The next lectures will
    introduce **First-Order Logic (FOL)**, also known as predicate
    calculus. FOL offers significantly greater expressive power by
    incorporating objects, relations, variables, and quantifiers
    ($\forall, \exists$). This allows for more compact and natural
    representation of general rules (e.g., \"All squares adjacent to a
    pit are breezy\") and potentially more elegant ways to handle
    reasoning about action and change, mitigating some of the
    representational burdens encountered in PL.
