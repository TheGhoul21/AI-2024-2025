Defining Artificial Intelligence: From Human Simulation to Rational Action

In our first lecture, we grappled with the definition of artificial intelligence. Initial attempts often equate AI to creating machines that simulate human intelligence. While intuitively appealing, this definition proves unsatisfactory due to the inherent difficulty in defining and measuring "intelligence" itself. Over time, the AI research community has shifted focus towards a more pragmatic and achievable goal: creating rational agents. The emphasis moved from mimicking human-like thought processes to designing agents that act optimally to achieve specific goals, regardless of whether their methods resemble human cognition.

This evolution in definition can be visualized through a framework considering two dimensions: thought processes vs. behavior and human-centered vs. rationality-centered approaches, as depicted in Figure 1.

The quadrant representing rational action (bottom right, quadrant 4 in Figure 1) embodies the modern, standard paradigm in AI. This approach prioritizes designing agents that make optimal decisions to achieve their objectives, even if these decision-making processes diverge from human-like thinking. This shift has yielded significant benefits, enabling the creation of AI systems that not only perform tasks effectively but, in some domains, surpass human capabilities. However, it also represents a departure from the original, perhaps more ambitious, goal of replicating human-level intelligence in machines. The concept of an agent, which acts within an environment, becomes central to this rational approach, setting the stage for today’s discussion.

Rational Agents: The Central Paradigm of Modern AI

Having established the foundational concepts of AI in the previous lectures, we now turn our attention to the central paradigm of modern AI: rational agents. This lecture will delve into the definition, architecture, and properties of rational agents, providing a framework for understanding and designing intelligent systems.

Why Agents?

The concept of an agent serves as a unifying abstraction in AI. It allows us to model diverse AI systems within a common framework, regardless of their specific tasks or underlying technologies. Thinking in terms of agents provides several conceptual advantages:

* Unified Perspective: The agent abstraction offers a homogeneous view of the AI field, encompassing various techniques and approaches under a single umbrella. This perspective, championed by Russell and Norvig in their seminal textbook, Artificial Intelligence: A Modern Approach (often referred to as "The Agent Book"), emphasizes the design of intelligent agents as the core objective of AI. * Rational Action Focus: The agent paradigm naturally aligns with the modern definition of AI, which prioritizes rationality. By focusing on agents that act rationally to achieve goals, we move away from the ambiguities of human-like intelligence and towards the design of systems that perform optimally in their environments. * Modular Design: The agent framework encourages a modular approach to AI system design. By clearly separating the agent from its environment, sensors, and actuators, we can design and analyze each component independently and then integrate them to create a complete intelligent system. * Broad Applicability: The agent concept is broadly applicable across diverse AI domains. From simple reflex agents to complex learning agents, and from single-agent systems to multi-agent systems, the agent abstraction provides a versatile tool for modeling and understanding intelligent behavior in various contexts.

Defining Agents and Rationality

An agent is formally defined as an entity that perceives and acts in an environment.

This seemingly simple definition encompasses a wide range of systems, from software programs to robots and even biological organisms. Key components of an agent definition are illustrated in Figure 2.

As shown in Figure 2, an agent interacts with its environment through sensors and actuators.

* Sensors are devices that allow the agent to perceive its environment. These can range from physical sensors like cameras and microphones for robots to software interfaces that receive input data for software agents. The agent’s perceptual input at any given time is called a percept. * Actuators are devices that allow the agent to act upon its environment. For robots, actuators might include motors, wheels, and manipulators. For software agents, actuators could be functions that modify data, display information, or send commands over a network. * The environment is the external world with which the agent interacts. It can be physical, like a room or a city, or virtual, like a game world or a database.

A rational agent is an agent that strives to maximize its expected utility.

At each point in time, a rational agent selects an action from the available options that it expects will lead to the best outcome, based on its current knowledge and beliefs. This concept of rationality is central to modern AI and distinguishes it from earlier approaches focused on mimicking human behavior.

It is crucial to note that rationality, in the context of AI agents, does not imply omniscience or perfection. A rational agent is expected to act optimally given its limitations, including:

* Limited Perception: Agents may have incomplete or noisy sensory information about their environment. For instance, humans can only perceive sounds within a specific frequency range, and visual perception can be obstructed. Similarly, a Roomba’s sensors might only detect obstacles immediately in its path, missing dust in corners it hasn’t reached yet. * Uncertainty: The outcomes of actions may be uncertain or probabilistic. For example, in a game like Pac-Man, the ghosts’ movements are not entirely predictable. Similarly, a self-driving car cannot be certain about the future actions of other drivers or pedestrians. * Computational Constraints: Agents may have limited computational resources and time to make decisions. A chess-playing AI, even with powerful hardware, cannot explore all possible game states within a reasonable timeframe. Real-time decision-making often requires agents to act under significant time pressure.

Therefore, a rational agent aims to maximize expected utility, acknowledging that perfect outcomes are not always achievable due to these inherent limitations. The design of rational agents involves developing mechanisms that enable them to make the best possible decisions under conditions of uncertainty and limited information.

Agent Function and Agent Program

To understand how an agent operates, we distinguish between two key concepts: the agent function and the agent program.

The agent function is a theoretical mapping from percept sequences to actions. Mathematically, it can be represented as: \[f: \mathcal{P}^* \rightarrow \mathcal{A}\] where \(\mathcal{P}^*\) is the set of all possible percept sequences (histories of all percepts received so far), and \(\mathcal{A}\) is the set of possible actions.

The agent function, in essence, specifies what action an agent should take in response to any possible history of perceptions. This function is an abstract ideal, defining the desired behavior of a rational agent in all circumstances. For example, in a chess game, the agent function would ideally map every possible sequence of moves (percepts) to the optimal next move (action).

The agent program is a concrete implementation of the agent function. It is the actual algorithm or code that runs inside the agent and determines its actions based on its percepts.

The agent program takes the current percept as input and any internal state maintained by the agent and outputs an action. It is a practical realization of the abstract agent function, often representing an approximation due to computational limitations and the complexity of implementing the ideal mapping for all possible percept sequences. For instance, a chess-playing program is an agent program that approximates the ideal agent function for chess, using algorithms and heuristics to choose moves without explicitly considering every possible game sequence.

Figure 3 illustrates the relationship between the agent function and the agent program within the agent architecture.

The agent program aims to implement the agent function, but it often does so approximately due to practical constraints. For instance, a simple agent program might only consider the most recent percept, ignoring the history of past percepts, while a more sophisticated program might maintain an internal state to remember relevant aspects of the percept history. The choice of agent program depends on factors such as the complexity of the environment, the desired level of rationality, and the available computational resources.

Table-Driven Agents: A Conceptual but Impractical Approach

One way to conceptualize the agent function is through a table-driven agent. In this approach, the agent function is explicitly represented as a lookup table. The table maps every possible percept sequence to the corresponding action that the agent should take. Table 1 shows a partial example for our toy vacuum cleaner agent.

Partial Table for Vacuum Cleaner Agent Function
Percept Sequence Location Action
\([Clean, A]\) A Right
\([Clean, B]\) B Left
\([Dirty, A]\) A Suck
\([Dirty, B]\) B Suck
\([Clean, A], [Clean, A]\) A Right
\([Clean, A], [Dirty, B]\) B Suck
... ... ...

Given a percept sequence, the table-driven agent simply looks up the corresponding action in the table and executes it. This approach is conceptually straightforward and guarantees rational behavior, provided that the table accurately represents the optimal agent function.

However, table-driven agents suffer from severe practical limitations due to combinatorial explosion. The number of possible percept sequences grows exponentially with the length of the sequence and the number of possible percepts.

Consider an environment with \(P\) possible percepts and an agent operating for \(T\) time steps. The number of possible percept sequences of length up to \(T\) is given by:

\[\sum_{i=1}^{T} P^i = \frac{P(P^T - 1)}{P - 1} \approx O(P^T)\]

For even moderately complex environments, the size of the lookup table becomes astronomically large, exceeding the memory capacity of any computer. For example, in a chess game, the number of possible game states is estimated to be around \(10^{40}\), and the number of possible game sequences is far larger, rendering a table-driven agent for chess completely infeasible. As mentioned in the transcript, for a game like chess, the table size could approach \(10^{150}\) entries, far exceeding the number of atoms in the universe.

Russell and Norvig propose the P-E-A-S framework as a structured method for specifying task environments. PEAS is an acronym for:

  • Performance Measure (P): This defines the criteria for success or failure of the agent’s actions. It specifies how the agent’s performance is to be evaluated. A well-defined performance measure is crucial for designing rational agents, as it guides the agent’s decision-making process towards maximizing this measure.

  • Environment (E): This specifies the world in which the agent operates. It includes the state space, the dynamics of the environment, and any relevant objects or entities within it.

  • Actuators (A): These are the means by which the agent can interact with and act upon the environment. Actuators define the set of actions the agent can perform.

  • Sensors (S): These are the means by which the agent perceives its environment. Sensors provide the agent with information about the current state of the environment, which it uses to make decisions.

The PEAS framework provides a structured approach to analyze and define any task environment, ensuring all crucial aspects for agent design are considered. Let’s illustrate this framework with several examples.

Detailed Examples of Task Environments and P-E-A-S Analysis

Applying the PEAS framework helps to concretely define the task environment for different types of agents. Let’s examine several examples discussed in the lecture.

Example 1: Toy Vacuum Cleaner Agent

Consider the toy vacuum cleaner agent operating in a simplified environment with two locations, A and B. We can define its task environment using PEAS as follows:

  • Performance Measure (P): Points are awarded for each clean location at each time step. A possible refinement, as discussed, could include penalizing the agent for each movement to encourage efficiency.

  • Environment (E): A discrete environment consisting of two locations, A and B. Each location can be either dirty or clean.

  • Actuators (A): The agent has three possible actions: Move Left, Move Right, and Suck (vacuum).

  • Sensors (S): The agent has two sensors: one to perceive its current location (A or B) and another to detect the dirt status of the current location (Dirty or Clean).

This PEAS description clearly outlines the task environment for the simple vacuum cleaner agent.

Example 2: Pac-Man Agent

For a Pac-Man agent playing the classic arcade game, the PEAS description becomes more complex:

  • Performance Measure (P): A more intricate scoring system is needed to capture the game’s objectives. A possible performance measure could be:

    • Points for each move: -1 (to encourage efficient play)

    • Points for eating a small pellet: +10

    • Points for winning a level (eating all pellets): +500

    • Penalty for dying (being eaten by a ghost): -500

    • Points for eating a ghost (after power pellet): +200

    This performance measure incentivizes the agent to eat pellets, win levels, and strategically eat ghosts while avoiding being caught.

  • Environment (E): The Pac-Man game world, including:

    • A grid of locations.

    • Pellets (small and large/power pellets) at certain locations.

    • Ghosts (with their positions and movement patterns).

    • Walls that restrict movement.

    The environment dynamics include ghost movement rules (which may be partially random or designed to chase Pac-Man) and the effect of power pellets on ghost behavior.

  • Actuators (A): The agent can move in four directions: Up, Down, Left, Right.

  • Sensors (S): The agent perceives:

    • The positions of all pellets (remaining and eaten).

    • The positions of all ghosts.

    • Its own current location.

    • Potentially, the remaining duration of the power pellet effect (though this might be uncertain).

The Pac-Man PEAS description highlights the increased complexity compared to the vacuum cleaner, particularly in the performance measure and environment definition.

Example 3: Autonomous Taxi Driving Agent

Designing an autonomous taxi requires a sophisticated task environment definition:

  • Performance Measure (P): This would likely involve multiple factors:

    • Monetary Earnings: Maximizing fares collected from passengers.

    • Passenger Satisfaction: Ensuring comfortable and safe rides, potentially measured through passenger feedback or proxies like ride duration and smoothness.

    • Operational Costs: Minimizing fuel consumption, vehicle wear and tear, and maintenance costs.

    • Safety and Legality: Avoiding accidents, traffic violations (fines), and ensuring passenger safety.

    • Reputation: Maintaining a good service reputation to attract more customers.

    Balancing these potentially conflicting objectives is a key challenge in designing the performance measure.

  • Environment (E): A complex, dynamic, and partially observable real-world environment, including:

    • Road networks (streets, highways, intersections, traffic lights, etc.).

    • Other vehicles (cars, motorcycles, bicycles, pedestrians).

    • Traffic rules and regulations.

    • Customers (passengers requesting rides, their destinations, and preferences).

    • Weather conditions (rain, snow, fog, etc.).

    • Traffic conditions (congestion, accidents, road closures).

    • Law enforcement (police).

    The environment is highly dynamic and unpredictable, with numerous external factors influencing the agent’s performance.

  • Actuators (A): The standard controls of a car:

    • Steering wheel (controlling direction).

    • Accelerator (controlling speed).

    • Brakes (controlling speed and stopping).

    • Turn signals.

    • Horn.

    • Display/interface for passenger interaction (e.g., showing route, playing media).

  • Sensors (S): A suite of sensors is necessary for perceiving the driving environment:

    • Cameras (for visual input, object detection, lane recognition).

    • Radar and Lidar (for distance sensing, obstacle detection).

    • GPS (for location and navigation).

    • Accelerometer and Inertial Measurement Unit (IMU) (for motion and orientation).

    • Engine sensors (for monitoring vehicle status).

    • Microphone (for potential voice commands from passengers).

The self-driving taxi example demonstrates the complexity of real-world task environments, requiring sophisticated sensors, actuators, and performance measures to address the challenges of autonomous navigation and decision-making in dynamic and uncertain conditions.

Further Examples: Medical Diagnosis System and Language Tutor

For a medical diagnosis system, the PEAS components can be described as:

  • Performance Measure (P): Patient health, treatment cost, and accuracy of diagnosis.

  • Environment (E): Patient, hospital, medical staff.

  • Actuators (A): Displaying diagnoses, recommending treatments, suggesting tests, alerting medical staff.

  • Sensors (S): Patient medical history, symptoms, test results, doctor’s notes.

For a language tutor, the PEAS components can be described as:

  • Performance Measure (P): Student test scores, student engagement, time to achieve fluency.

  • Environment (E): Student, learning platform, exercises, language content.

  • Actuators (A): Providing exercises, giving feedback, offering hints, adapting difficulty, presenting new content.

  • Sensors (S): Student responses, performance data, time spent learning, student self-assessment.

These examples further illustrate the versatility of the PEAS framework in capturing diverse AI applications.

Properties of Task Environments: Classifying Environmental Characteristics

To further understand and categorize task environments, we can analyze them based on several key properties. These properties help in selecting appropriate agent architectures and algorithms for different tasks. We will now discuss some of these properties, starting with observability.

Observability: Distinguishing Between Fully and Partially Observable Environments

Observability refers to the degree to which an agent’s sensors can perceive the current state of the environment.

Task environments can be categorized as:

  • Fully Observable: In a fully observable environment, the agent’s sensors provide it with access to the complete state of the environment at each point in time. The agent knows everything relevant to making optimal decisions. Examples of approximately fully observable environments include:

    • Chess (with perfect information): The agent can see the entire chessboard, including the positions of all pieces.

    • Vacuum Cleaner in a simple, discretized environment (as initially described): If the agent always knows its location and the dirt status of both locations, it is a fully observable environment.

    In fully observable environments, agents do not need to maintain internal state to track aspects of the environment not directly perceived, as all relevant information is immediately available through sensors.

  • Partially Observable: In a partially observable environment, the agent’s sensors only provide limited or incomplete information about the environment’s state. The agent may not have direct access to all aspects relevant to its decision-making. Most real-world environments are partially observable. Examples include:

    • Pac-Man: While Pac-Man perceives the locations of pellets and ghosts in its vicinity, it might not have perfect information about the ghosts’ future movements or intentions.

    • Self-driving car: Sensors provide a view of the surroundings, but there are occlusions, limited sensor range, and uncertainties about the behavior of other agents (drivers, pedestrians). The agent does not have complete knowledge of the state of all traffic participants or road conditions at all times.

    • Vacuum cleaner in a more realistic setting: A Roomba might not know the dirt distribution in rooms it hasn’t yet explored or behind furniture. It relies on its local sensors and memory of visited areas to infer cleanliness.

    In partially observable environments, agents often need to maintain internal state to keep track of aspects of the environment that are not directly sensed. They may need to infer the hidden parts of the environment based on past percepts and their understanding of the environment dynamics. This often involves using memory, belief states, and probabilistic reasoning to make informed decisions under uncertainty.

  • Unobservable: In a completely unobservable environment (a theoretical extreme), the agent receives no sensory information about the environment at all. While practically rare, this extreme case helps to understand the spectrum of observability. In such scenarios, agents must operate based solely on prior knowledge or pre-programmed behaviors, without any feedback from the environment.

The degree of observability significantly impacts the design of rational agents. Agents in fully observable environments can often employ simpler decision-making strategies, as they have complete information. Agents in partially observable environments require more sophisticated techniques to handle uncertainty and incomplete information, often involving state estimation, prediction, and memory.

Conclusion

In today’s lecture, we introduced the concept of rational agents as a central paradigm in modern AI. We started by refining our understanding of AI, shifting from mere human simulation to the design of systems capable of rational action. We explored the historical context that led to this paradigm shift and discussed the current state and future perspectives of AI, highlighting its growing capabilities and ethical considerations.

We delved into the definition of agents, emphasizing the principle of rationality, and examined the architecture of agents in terms of sensors, actuators, and their interaction with the environment. We discussed the agent function and program, focusing on how agents map perceptions to actions, and introduced the table-driven agent as a conceptual model, while also acknowledging its practical limitations due to computational complexity. Using the toy vacuum cleaner example, we illustrated these concepts in a simple environment. We underscored the importance of autonomy and learning for truly intelligent agents, contrasting them with purely reactive systems.

Furthermore, we explored the concept of task environments using the PEAS framework (Performance, Environment, Actuators, Sensors). Through detailed examples like the vacuum cleaner, Pac-Man, autonomous taxi driving, medical diagnosis systems, and foreign language tutoring systems, we demonstrated how to analyze and specify different operational domains for agents. Finally, we began to classify task environments based on their properties, starting with observability, distinguishing between fully and partially observable environments.

Important Remarks and Key Takeaways:

  • Rationality over Human Simulation: We emphasized the modern AI paradigm shift towards designing rational agents that optimize performance based on clearly defined objectives, rather than merely attempting to replicate human-like intelligence. This focus on rationality provides a more concrete and measurable goal for AI development.

  • Agent-Centric View: The agent abstraction provides a powerful and unifying framework for understanding and designing intelligent systems. By viewing AI systems as agents interacting with environments, we gain a consistent perspective applicable across diverse AI domains and techniques.

  • Task Environment is Crucial: Defining the task environment using the PEAS framework is an essential first step in designing any intelligent agent. The PEAS framework ensures that all critical aspects of the problem, including performance metrics, environmental characteristics, and agent capabilities, are systematically considered.

  • Practical Limitations of Table-Driven Agents: While conceptually illustrative, table-driven agents highlight the challenge of combinatorial explosion. The exponential growth in the number of possible percept sequences renders this approach impractical for real-world problems, necessitating more efficient and scalable agent architectures.

  • Observability Impacts Agent Design: The observability of the task environment is a fundamental property that significantly influences agent design. Moving from fully observable to partially observable environments necessitates increasingly sophisticated agent programs capable of handling uncertainty, incomplete information, and the need for internal state and inference.

# Rational Agents: The Central Paradigm of Modern AI

Having established the foundational concepts of AI in the previous
lectures, we now turn our attention to the central paradigm of modern
AI: **rational agents**. This lecture will delve into the definition,
architecture, and properties of rational agents, providing a framework
for understanding and designing intelligent systems.

## Why Agents?

The concept of an agent serves as a unifying abstraction in AI. It
allows us to model diverse AI systems within a common framework,
regardless of their specific tasks or underlying technologies. Thinking
in terms of agents provides several conceptual advantages:

\* **Unified Perspective:** The agent abstraction offers a homogeneous
view of the AI field, encompassing various techniques and approaches
under a single umbrella. This perspective, championed by Russell and
Norvig in their seminal textbook, *Artificial Intelligence: A Modern
Approach* (often referred to as \"The Agent Book\"), emphasizes the
design of intelligent agents as the core objective of AI. \* **Rational
Action Focus:** The agent paradigm naturally aligns with the modern
definition of AI, which prioritizes *rationality*. By focusing on agents
that act rationally to achieve goals, we move away from the ambiguities
of human-like intelligence and towards the design of systems that
perform optimally in their environments. \* **Modular Design:** The
agent framework encourages a modular approach to AI system design. By
clearly separating the agent from its environment, sensors, and
actuators, we can design and analyze each component independently and
then integrate them to create a complete intelligent system. \* **Broad
Applicability:** The agent concept is broadly applicable across diverse
AI domains. From simple reflex agents to complex learning agents, and
from single-agent systems to multi-agent systems, the agent abstraction
provides a versatile tool for modeling and understanding intelligent
behavior in various contexts.

## Defining Agents and Rationality

::: tcolorbox
An **agent** is formally defined as an **entity that perceives and
acts** in an environment.
:::

This seemingly simple definition encompasses a wide range of systems,
from software programs to robots and even biological organisms. Key
components of an agent definition are illustrated in Figure
[2](#fig:agent_schema){reference-type="ref"
reference="fig:agent_schema"}.

<figure id="fig:agent_schema">

<figcaption>Agent Interaction with Environment</figcaption>
</figure>

As shown in Figure [2](#fig:agent_schema){reference-type="ref"
reference="fig:agent_schema"}, an agent interacts with its
**environment** through **sensors** and **actuators**.

\* **Sensors** are devices that allow the agent to perceive its
environment. These can range from physical sensors like cameras and
microphones for robots to software interfaces that receive input data
for software agents. The agent's perceptual input at any given time is
called a **percept**. \* **Actuators** are devices that allow the agent
to act upon its environment. For robots, actuators might include motors,
wheels, and manipulators. For software agents, actuators could be
functions that modify data, display information, or send commands over a
network. \* The **environment** is the external world with which the
agent interacts. It can be physical, like a room or a city, or virtual,
like a game world or a database.

::: tcolorbox
A **rational agent** is an agent that strives to **maximize its expected
utility**.
:::

At each point in time, a rational agent selects an action from the
available options that it expects will lead to the best outcome, based
on its current knowledge and beliefs. This concept of rationality is
central to modern AI and distinguishes it from earlier approaches
focused on mimicking human behavior.

::: tcolorbox
It is crucial to note that rationality, in the context of AI agents,
does not imply omniscience or perfection. A rational agent is expected
to act optimally **given its limitations**, including:

\* **Limited Perception:** Agents may have incomplete or noisy sensory
information about their environment. For instance, humans can only
perceive sounds within a specific frequency range, and visual perception
can be obstructed. Similarly, a Roomba's sensors might only detect
obstacles immediately in its path, missing dust in corners it hasn't
reached yet. \* **Uncertainty:** The outcomes of actions may be
uncertain or probabilistic. For example, in a game like Pac-Man, the
ghosts' movements are not entirely predictable. Similarly, a
self-driving car cannot be certain about the future actions of other
drivers or pedestrians. \* **Computational Constraints:** Agents may
have limited computational resources and time to make decisions. A
chess-playing AI, even with powerful hardware, cannot explore all
possible game states within a reasonable timeframe. Real-time
decision-making often requires agents to act under significant time
pressure.
:::

Therefore, a rational agent aims to maximize **expected utility**,
acknowledging that perfect outcomes are not always achievable due to
these inherent limitations. The design of rational agents involves
developing mechanisms that enable them to make the best possible
decisions under conditions of uncertainty and limited information.

## Agent Function and Agent Program

To understand how an agent operates, we distinguish between two key
concepts: the **agent function** and the **agent program**.

::: tcolorbox
The **agent function** is a theoretical mapping from percept sequences
to actions. Mathematically, it can be represented as:
$$f: \mathcal{P}^* \rightarrow \mathcal{A}$$ where $\mathcal{P}^*$ is
the set of all possible percept sequences (histories of all percepts
received so far), and $\mathcal{A}$ is the set of possible actions.
:::

The agent function, in essence, specifies what action an agent should
take in response to any possible history of perceptions. This function
is an abstract ideal, defining the desired behavior of a rational agent
in all circumstances. For example, in a chess game, the agent function
would ideally map every possible sequence of moves (percepts) to the
optimal next move (action).

::: tcolorbox
The **agent program** is a concrete implementation of the agent
function. It is the actual algorithm or code that runs inside the agent
and determines its actions based on its percepts.
:::

The agent program takes the current percept as input and any internal
state maintained by the agent and outputs an action. It is a practical
realization of the abstract agent function, often representing an
approximation due to computational limitations and the complexity of
implementing the ideal mapping for all possible percept sequences. For
instance, a chess-playing program is an agent program that approximates
the ideal agent function for chess, using algorithms and heuristics to
choose moves without explicitly considering every possible game
sequence.

Figure [3](#fig:agent_schema_2){reference-type="ref"
reference="fig:agent_schema_2"} illustrates the relationship between the
agent function and the agent program within the agent architecture.

<figure id="fig:agent_schema_2">

<figcaption>Agent Function within Agent</figcaption>
</figure>

The agent program aims to implement the agent function, but it often
does so approximately due to practical constraints. For instance, a
simple agent program might only consider the most recent percept,
ignoring the history of past percepts, while a more sophisticated
program might maintain an internal state to remember relevant aspects of
the percept history. The choice of agent program depends on factors such
as the complexity of the environment, the desired level of rationality,
and the available computational resources.

## Table-Driven Agents: A Conceptual but Impractical Approach

One way to conceptualize the agent function is through a **table-driven
agent**. In this approach, the agent function is explicitly represented
as a lookup table. The table maps every possible percept sequence to the
corresponding action that the agent should take. Table
[1](#tab:vacuum_agent_table){reference-type="ref"
reference="tab:vacuum_agent_table"} shows a partial example for our toy
vacuum cleaner agent.

::: tab:vacuum_agent_table
       Percept Sequence       Location   Action
  -------------------------- ---------- --------
         $[Clean, A]$            A       Right
         $[Clean, B]$            B        Left
         $[Dirty, A]$            A        Suck
         $[Dirty, B]$            B        Suck
   $[Clean, A], [Clean, A]$      A       Right
   $[Clean, A], [Dirty, B]$      B        Suck
             \...               \...      \...

  : Partial Table for Vacuum Cleaner Agent Function
:::

Given a percept sequence, the table-driven agent simply looks up the
corresponding action in the table and executes it. This approach is
conceptually straightforward and guarantees rational behavior, provided
that the table accurately represents the optimal agent function.

::: tcolorbox
However, table-driven agents suffer from severe practical limitations
due to **combinatorial explosion**. The number of possible percept
sequences grows exponentially with the length of the sequence and the
number of possible percepts.
:::

Consider an environment with $P$ possible percepts and an agent
operating for $T$ time steps. The number of possible percept sequences
of length up to $T$ is given by:

$$\sum_{i=1}^{T} P^i = \frac{P(P^T - 1)}{P - 1} \approx O(P^T)$$

::: tcolorbox
For even moderately complex environments, the size of the lookup table
becomes astronomically large, exceeding the memory capacity of any
computer. For example, in a chess game, the number of possible game
states is estimated to be around $10^{40}$, and the number of possible
game sequences is far larger, rendering a table-driven agent for chess
completely infeasible. As mentioned in the transcript, for a game like
chess, the table size could approach $10^{150}$ entries, far exceeding
the number of atoms in the universe.
:::

::: tcolorbox
Russell and Norvig propose the **P-E-A-S** framework as a structured
method for specifying task environments. PEAS is an acronym for:

-   **Performance Measure (P):** This defines the criteria for success
    or failure of the agent's actions. It specifies how the agent's
    performance is to be evaluated. A well-defined performance measure
    is crucial for designing rational agents, as it guides the agent's
    decision-making process towards maximizing this measure.

-   **Environment (E):** This specifies the world in which the agent
    operates. It includes the state space, the dynamics of the
    environment, and any relevant objects or entities within it.

-   **Actuators (A):** These are the means by which the agent can
    interact with and act upon the environment. Actuators define the set
    of actions the agent can perform.

-   **Sensors (S):** These are the means by which the agent perceives
    its environment. Sensors provide the agent with information about
    the current state of the environment, which it uses to make
    decisions.
:::

The PEAS framework provides a structured approach to analyze and define
any task environment, ensuring all crucial aspects for agent design are
considered. Let's illustrate this framework with several examples.

## Detailed Examples of Task Environments and P-E-A-S Analysis

Applying the PEAS framework helps to concretely define the task
environment for different types of agents. Let's examine several
examples discussed in the lecture.

### Example 1: Toy Vacuum Cleaner Agent

::: tcolorbox
Consider the toy vacuum cleaner agent operating in a simplified
environment with two locations, A and B. We can define its task
environment using PEAS as follows:

-   **Performance Measure (P):** Points are awarded for each clean
    location at each time step. A possible refinement, as discussed,
    could include penalizing the agent for each movement to encourage
    efficiency.

-   **Environment (E):** A discrete environment consisting of two
    locations, A and B. Each location can be either dirty or clean.

-   **Actuators (A):** The agent has three possible actions: *Move
    Left*, *Move Right*, and *Suck* (vacuum).

-   **Sensors (S):** The agent has two sensors: one to perceive its
    *current location* (A or B) and another to detect the *dirt status*
    of the current location (Dirty or Clean).
:::

This PEAS description clearly outlines the task environment for the
simple vacuum cleaner agent.

### Example 2: Pac-Man Agent

::: tcolorbox
For a Pac-Man agent playing the classic arcade game, the PEAS
description becomes more complex:

-   **Performance Measure (P):** A more intricate scoring system is
    needed to capture the game's objectives. A possible performance
    measure could be:

    -   **Points for each move:** -1 (to encourage efficient play)

    -   **Points for eating a small pellet:** +10

    -   **Points for winning a level (eating all pellets):** +500

    -   **Penalty for dying (being eaten by a ghost):** -500

    -   **Points for eating a ghost (after power pellet):** +200

    This performance measure incentivizes the agent to eat pellets, win
    levels, and strategically eat ghosts while avoiding being caught.

-   **Environment (E):** The Pac-Man game world, including:

    -   A grid of locations.

    -   Pellets (small and large/power pellets) at certain locations.

    -   Ghosts (with their positions and movement patterns).

    -   Walls that restrict movement.

    The environment dynamics include ghost movement rules (which may be
    partially random or designed to chase Pac-Man) and the effect of
    power pellets on ghost behavior.

-   **Actuators (A):** The agent can move in *four directions*: Up,
    Down, Left, Right.

-   **Sensors (S):** The agent perceives:

    -   The positions of all pellets (remaining and eaten).

    -   The positions of all ghosts.

    -   Its own current location.

    -   Potentially, the remaining duration of the power pellet effect
        (though this might be uncertain).
:::

The Pac-Man PEAS description highlights the increased complexity
compared to the vacuum cleaner, particularly in the performance measure
and environment definition.

### Example 3: Autonomous Taxi Driving Agent

::: tcolorbox
Designing an autonomous taxi requires a sophisticated task environment
definition:

-   **Performance Measure (P):** This would likely involve multiple
    factors:

    -   **Monetary Earnings:** Maximizing fares collected from
        passengers.

    -   **Passenger Satisfaction:** Ensuring comfortable and safe rides,
        potentially measured through passenger feedback or proxies like
        ride duration and smoothness.

    -   **Operational Costs:** Minimizing fuel consumption, vehicle wear
        and tear, and maintenance costs.

    -   **Safety and Legality:** Avoiding accidents, traffic violations
        (fines), and ensuring passenger safety.

    -   **Reputation:** Maintaining a good service reputation to attract
        more customers.

    Balancing these potentially conflicting objectives is a key
    challenge in designing the performance measure.

-   **Environment (E):** A complex, dynamic, and partially observable
    real-world environment, including:

    -   Road networks (streets, highways, intersections, traffic lights,
        etc.).

    -   Other vehicles (cars, motorcycles, bicycles, pedestrians).

    -   Traffic rules and regulations.

    -   Customers (passengers requesting rides, their destinations, and
        preferences).

    -   Weather conditions (rain, snow, fog, etc.).

    -   Traffic conditions (congestion, accidents, road closures).

    -   Law enforcement (police).

    The environment is highly dynamic and unpredictable, with numerous
    external factors influencing the agent's performance.

-   **Actuators (A):** The standard controls of a car:

    -   Steering wheel (controlling direction).

    -   Accelerator (controlling speed).

    -   Brakes (controlling speed and stopping).

    -   Turn signals.

    -   Horn.

    -   Display/interface for passenger interaction (e.g., showing
        route, playing media).

-   **Sensors (S):** A suite of sensors is necessary for perceiving the
    driving environment:

    -   Cameras (for visual input, object detection, lane recognition).

    -   Radar and Lidar (for distance sensing, obstacle detection).

    -   GPS (for location and navigation).

    -   Accelerometer and Inertial Measurement Unit (IMU) (for motion
        and orientation).

    -   Engine sensors (for monitoring vehicle status).

    -   Microphone (for potential voice commands from passengers).
:::

The self-driving taxi example demonstrates the complexity of real-world
task environments, requiring sophisticated sensors, actuators, and
performance measures to address the challenges of autonomous navigation
and decision-making in dynamic and uncertain conditions.

### Further Examples: Medical Diagnosis System and Language Tutor

::: tcolorbox
For a medical diagnosis system, the PEAS components can be described as:

-   **Performance Measure (P):** Patient health, treatment cost, and
    accuracy of diagnosis.

-   **Environment (E):** Patient, hospital, medical staff.

-   **Actuators (A):** Displaying diagnoses, recommending treatments,
    suggesting tests, alerting medical staff.

-   **Sensors (S):** Patient medical history, symptoms, test results,
    doctor's notes.
:::

::: tcolorbox
For a language tutor, the PEAS components can be described as:

-   **Performance Measure (P):** Student test scores, student
    engagement, time to achieve fluency.

-   **Environment (E):** Student, learning platform, exercises, language
    content.

-   **Actuators (A):** Providing exercises, giving feedback, offering
    hints, adapting difficulty, presenting new content.

-   **Sensors (S):** Student responses, performance data, time spent
    learning, student self-assessment.
:::

These examples further illustrate the versatility of the PEAS framework
in capturing diverse AI applications.

## Properties of Task Environments: Classifying Environmental Characteristics

To further understand and categorize task environments, we can analyze
them based on several key properties. These properties help in selecting
appropriate agent architectures and algorithms for different tasks. We
will now discuss some of these properties, starting with observability.

### Observability: Distinguishing Between Fully and Partially Observable Environments

::: tcolorbox
**Observability** refers to the degree to which an agent's sensors can
perceive the current state of the environment.
:::

Task environments can be categorized as:

-   **Fully Observable:** In a fully observable environment, the agent's
    sensors provide it with access to the complete state of the
    environment at each point in time. The agent knows everything
    relevant to making optimal decisions. Examples of approximately
    fully observable environments include:

    -   **Chess (with perfect information):** The agent can see the
        entire chessboard, including the positions of all pieces.

    -   **Vacuum Cleaner in a simple, discretized environment (as
        initially described):** If the agent always knows its location
        and the dirt status of both locations, it is a fully observable
        environment.

    In fully observable environments, agents do not need to maintain
    internal state to track aspects of the environment not directly
    perceived, as all relevant information is immediately available
    through sensors.

-   **Partially Observable:** In a partially observable environment, the
    agent's sensors only provide limited or incomplete information about
    the environment's state. The agent may not have direct access to all
    aspects relevant to its decision-making. Most real-world
    environments are partially observable. Examples include:

    -   **Pac-Man:** While Pac-Man perceives the locations of pellets
        and ghosts in its vicinity, it might not have perfect
        information about the ghosts' future movements or intentions.

    -   **Self-driving car:** Sensors provide a view of the
        surroundings, but there are occlusions, limited sensor range,
        and uncertainties about the behavior of other agents (drivers,
        pedestrians). The agent does not have complete knowledge of the
        state of all traffic participants or road conditions at all
        times.

    -   **Vacuum cleaner in a more realistic setting:** A Roomba might
        not know the dirt distribution in rooms it hasn't yet explored
        or behind furniture. It relies on its local sensors and memory
        of visited areas to infer cleanliness.

    In partially observable environments, agents often need to maintain
    internal state to keep track of aspects of the environment that are
    not directly sensed. They may need to infer the hidden parts of the
    environment based on past percepts and their understanding of the
    environment dynamics. This often involves using memory, belief
    states, and probabilistic reasoning to make informed decisions under
    uncertainty.

-   **Unobservable:** In a completely unobservable environment (a
    theoretical extreme), the agent receives no sensory information
    about the environment at all. While practically rare, this extreme
    case helps to understand the spectrum of observability. In such
    scenarios, agents must operate based solely on prior knowledge or
    pre-programmed behaviors, without any feedback from the environment.

The degree of observability significantly impacts the design of rational
agents. Agents in fully observable environments can often employ simpler
decision-making strategies, as they have complete information. Agents in
partially observable environments require more sophisticated techniques
to handle uncertainty and incomplete information, often involving state
estimation, prediction, and memory.

    **combinatorial explosion**. The exponential growth in the number of
    possible percept sequences renders this approach impractical for
    real-world problems, necessitating more efficient and scalable agent
    architectures.

-   **Observability Impacts Agent Design:** The **observability** of the
    task environment is a fundamental property that significantly
    influences agent design. Moving from fully observable to partially
    observable environments necessitates increasingly sophisticated
    agent programs capable of handling uncertainty, incomplete
    information, and the need for internal state and inference.
:::
