Introduction

This lecture serves as an introduction to the field of Artificial Intelligence (Artificial Intelligence) and to this course. Our discussion today will be twofold: first, we will address practical matters concerning the course itself, including information about the instructors, the course structure, required materials, and the examination process. Second, and more importantly, we will embark on an exploration of the very definition of Artificial Intelligence. This involves examining how Artificial Intelligenceis perceived in popular culture and media, contrasting these perceptions with expert opinions, and ultimately delving into more formal and nuanced definitions of the field.

This introductory session is designed to provide a comprehensive overview of Artificial Intelligenceas a discipline. We aim to establish a foundational understanding that will prepare you for the more technical and in-depth explorations of Artificial Intelligencetechniques and concepts that will follow in subsequent lectures. We will touch upon the core motivations that drive the study and development of Artificial Intelligence, briefly trace the historical trajectory of Artificial Intelligencefrom its inception to the present day, and consider the current landscape of Artificial Intelligence, paying particular attention to recent paradigm shifts that are reshaping the field. By the end of this lecture, you should have a clearer understanding of what Artificial Intelligenceis, why it is important, and what to expect from this course.

Defining Artificial Intelligence

Initial Perceptions and Misconceptions of AI

Professor Mizero initiated the discussion on defining Artificial Intelligenceby exploring common initial perceptions, which are often heavily influenced by popular culture and mass media. These sources frequently shape public understanding, sometimes leading to misconceptions about the true nature and current state of Artificial Intelligence.

AI in Popular Culture and Media: Apocalyptic Visions and Hype

In popular culture, particularly in films, Artificial Intelligenceis often portrayed through apocalyptic or dystopian lenses. These narratives commonly feature robots or advanced computer systems that evolve into threats to humanity. Examples abound in cinematic depictions where Artificial Intelligenceentities seek to dominate or even destroy the world, creating dystopian futures where machines rise against their creators. While such portrayals are undeniably dramatic and captivating for entertainment purposes, they frequently offer an unrealistic and often sensationalized perspective on Artificial Intelligence. It is crucial to recognize that these are fictional constructs designed to engage audiences and explore dramatic themes, rather than accurate reflections of Artificial Intelligence’s current capabilities or developmental trajectory.

Professor Mizero highlighted HAL9000 from 2001: A Space Odyssey as a significant example. HAL9000 illustrates that Artificial Intelligencein popular culture is not always represented by physical robots. It can also manifest as a disembodied software entity, a sophisticated computer system capable of intelligence and, in this case, malevolence. This example broadens the understanding of how Artificial Intelligenceis imagined in the public sphere, extending beyond just robotic forms to encompass intelligent software agents.

Mass media and news outlets also contribute to the public perception of Artificial Intelligence, often focusing on aspects such as substantial financial investments and funding in Artificial Intelligenceresearch and development. Reports frequently discuss the broad societal impact of Artificial Intelligence, encompassing both potential benefits and risks. Concerns are often raised regarding job displacement due to increasing automation driven by Artificial Intelligence, and the ethical dilemmas associated with Artificial Intelligence-driven decision-making processes, such as in automated hiring systems or algorithmic firing protocols. The potential for inherent biases within Artificial Intelligencesystems, which can lead to unfair or discriminatory outcomes across various applications, is another recurring and critical theme in media discussions. Professor Mizero referenced the example of a recidivism prediction system deployed in the United States that exhibited racial bias, disproportionately categorizing people of color as higher risk, illustrating the real-world implications of bias in Artificial Intelligence.

Furthermore, news coverage of Artificial Intelligenceis often characterized by hype and sensationalism. Media outlets may exaggerate the current capabilities and near-future impact of Artificial Intelligencetechnologies, sometimes presenting overly optimistic or alarmist scenarios. This tendency to sensationalize Artificial Intelligence, while perhaps serving to capture public attention, can also distort a balanced understanding of the field. Professor Mizero pointed out that even experts within the Artificial Intelligencecommunity are not entirely immune to contributing to this cycle of hype. Historically, there have been instances, even in the early days of Artificial Intelligenceresearch, where researchers may have oversold the immediate potential and near-term achievements of Artificial Intelligence, leading to periods of inflated expectations followed by disillusionment when those expectations were not rapidly met. This historical context is important for understanding the cyclical nature of Artificial Intelligencehype and the need for a more grounded and realistic perspective.

Expert Views vs. Public Perception: A Grounded Perspective

To provide a counterpoint to the often sensationalized media portrayals, Professor Mizero presented a more balanced and grounded perspective derived from Russell & Norvig, the authors of the course’s textbook, Artificial Intelligence: A Modern Approach. Russell & Norvigoffer a view that tempers the extremes often seen in popular culture. They acknowledge that while Artificial Intelligencemay not yet have reached the level of sophistication depicted in science fiction films, it has nonetheless achieved remarkable progress and yielded substantial practical results across a diverse range of domains. Russell & Norvigargue that while the current capabilities of Artificial Intelligencemight not be as overtly dramatic or sensational as media hype often suggests, they are, in reality, quite significant and continue to have a growing impact on society and technology. This expert perspective emphasizes the tangible advancements and real-world applications of Artificial Intelligence, advocating for a more informed and less sensationalized understanding of the field’s current state and future potential.

A Framework for Understanding AI: Four Definitional Approaches

To further refine the definition of Artificial Intelligenceand provide a structured framework for understanding its various facets, Professor Mizero introduced a categorization scheme adapted from Russell & Norvig. This framework organizes different approaches to defining Artificial Intelligencealong two key dimensions, creating a 2x2 matrix of definitional perspectives. These dimensions help to clarify the underlying assumptions and goals that shape different conceptions of what Artificial Intelligenceis and what it should strive to achieve.

The two dimensions structuring this framework are:

  • Focus on Thought Processes (Thinking) vs. Observable Behavior (Acting): This dimension distinguishes between definitions that prioritize the internal cognitive mechanisms of Artificial Intelligencesystems (thinking) and those that focus on the externally observable actions and performance of these systems (acting). Definitions emphasizing "thinking" are concerned with replicating or modeling the internal processes of intelligence, while definitions emphasizing "acting" are more concerned with achieving intelligent behavior, regardless of the underlying mechanism.

  • Goal of Human-like Performance (Human-like) vs. Ideal or Optimal Performance (Rational): This dimension differentiates between definitions that aim to create Artificial Intelligencesystems that perform tasks in a way that is similar to humans (human-like) and those that aim to achieve the best possible or most rational outcome, even if it deviates from typical human behavior (rational or optimal). The "human-like" goal emphasizes imitation of human intelligence, while the "rational" goal prioritizes effectiveness and optimality, potentially exceeding human limitations.

By crossing these two dimensions, Russell & Norvigand Professor Mizero presented four distinct approaches to defining Artificial Intelligence, each representing a different emphasis and set of objectives. These four approaches are:

Acting Humanly: The Turing Test and Behavioral Mimicry

This approach to defining Artificial Intelligencecenters on the goal of constructing systems that exhibit behavior indistinguishable from that of a human. The foundational concept for this approach is the Turing Test, proposed by Alan Turing in his seminal 1950 paper.

Turing Test: The Turing Test is defined as a test of a machine’s capacity to demonstrate intelligent behavior that is equivalent to, or indistinguishable from, that of a human. In the original setup envisioned by Turing, a human evaluator engages in natural language conversations with two entities, both hidden from view. One entity is a human, and the other is a machine. The evaluator’s task is to determine, solely based on the conversational exchanges, which entity is the machine and which is the human. If the evaluator, after a sustained conversation, cannot reliably differentiate the machine from the human, then the machine is considered to have passed the Turing Test. Passing this test is interpreted as evidence that the machine has achieved a level of intelligence comparable to a human, at least in the context of natural language interaction.

For a machine to successfully pass the Turing Test, it must possess a range of sophisticated capabilities, encompassing several key areas of Artificial Intelligence:

  • Natural Language Processing (NLP): Essential for enabling the machine to communicate effectively in human language. This includes the ability to understand natural language input (questions, statements) and generate coherent and contextually appropriate natural language responses. NLP is crucial for the machine to engage in meaningful conversation and convincingly mimic human linguistic abilities.

  • Knowledge Representation: The machine needs robust knowledge representation mechanisms to store and represent information about the world. This knowledge base is necessary for the machine to draw upon relevant facts, concepts, and relationships to answer questions, provide context, and participate in conversations in an informed manner. Effective knowledge representation allows the machine to access and utilize information as humans do.

  • Automated Reasoning: To utilize the stored knowledge effectively, the machine must possess automated reasoning capabilities. This involves the ability to use stored information to answer questions logically, draw conclusions from given premises, and make inferences. Automated reasoning allows the machine to process information, solve problems, and generate new knowledge from existing knowledge, mimicking human cognitive processes.

  • Machine Learning (ML): Crucially, the machine should incorporate machine learning to adapt to new information and improve over time. Learning allows the machine to refine its responses, expand its knowledge base, and become more adept at conversation through experience. Machine learning is essential for creating systems that are not static but can evolve and enhance their performance, mirroring human learning and adaptation.

Turing also conceived of an extended version of the test, often referred to as the Total Turing Test, which adds further requirements for embodied intelligence and physical interaction:

  • Computer Vision: For a Total Turing Test, the machine would need computer vision capabilities to perceive the world visually. This would enable the machine to process and interpret visual information from its environment, similar to human visual perception. Vision is a crucial sensory modality for humans, and incorporating it into the Turing Test expands the scope of intelligent behavior being assessed.

  • Robotics: Furthermore, the Total Turing Test would necessitate robotics capabilities, allowing the machine to physically interact with and manipulate objects in the world. This embodiment would enable the machine to not only converse intelligently but also act intelligently within a physical environment, further blurring the lines between machine and human behavior. Robotics adds the dimension of physical agency and interaction to the assessment of machine intelligence.

Professor Mizero emphasized that while the Turing Test remains a highly influential and conceptually significant benchmark in the field of Artificial Intelligence, it also has inherent limitations. One key limitation is that a system might potentially pass the test through clever simulation and mimicry of human conversation patterns, without possessing genuine "intelligence" or deep understanding. Such a system might rely on sophisticated pattern matching and pre-programmed responses rather than true cognitive processing. Furthermore, in domains where Artificial Intelligencehas already surpassed human capabilities, such as in chess or medical diagnosis, the goal of solely mimicking human behavior might be counterproductive. In these scenarios, aiming for human-level performance might actually mean intentionally limiting the system’s potential. For instance, to appear convincingly human in a medical diagnosis Turing Test, an Artificial Intelligencesystem might need to intentionally make mistakes at a rate comparable to human doctors, which is clearly not the desired objective in real-world medical applications. This highlights that while acting humanly is one approach to defining Artificial Intelligence, it is not without its drawbacks and may not always align with the ultimate goals of creating truly intelligent and beneficial systems.

Thinking Humanly: Cognitive Modeling and Understanding Human Thought Processes

This second approach to defining Artificial Intelligenceshifts the focus from external behavior to internal cognitive processes. The goal here is to build systems that not only act like humans but also think like humans. This approach, known as cognitive modeling, centers on developing computational models of human thought processes. It involves a deep investigation into the workings of the human mind, aiming to understand and ultimately replicate the internal mechanisms of human cognition.

To achieve this, researchers in cognitive modeling employ various methods to study and analyze human thought processes:

  • Introspection and Self-Analysis: One method involves introspection, where researchers analyze their own thought processes. This subjective approach involves careful self-observation and reflection on one’s own cognitive experiences. While introspection can provide valuable insights, it is also inherently limited by its subjective nature and the potential for biases in self-reporting.

  • Psychological Experiments and Behavioral Studies: A more empirical approach involves conducting psychological experiments and behavioral studies to understand human cognitive functions. These experiments are designed to probe specific aspects of human cognition, such as perception, memory, language, and reasoning. Professor Mizero cited the example of the Bouba-Kiki effect as an illustration of psychological experimentation.

    Bouba-Kiki Effect: The Bouba-Kiki effect is a psychological phenomenon demonstrating a non-arbitrary mapping between speech sounds and visual shapes. In this experiment, participants are typically shown two shapes: one rounded and curvilinear, and the other angular and spiky. They are then asked to associate each shape with one of two pseudowords: "Bouba" and "Kiki." Across diverse cultures and languages, a strong majority of participants consistently associate the rounded shape with "Bouba" and the spiky shape with "Kiki." This effect suggests that human sound symbolism and cross-modal sensory correspondence play a role in shaping our intuitive associations between language and visual forms. Experiments like this provide insights into the underlying principles of human cognition and perception.

    Such experiments, like the Bouba-Kiki effect study, provide empirical data that can inform the development of cognitive models and help researchers understand the underlying principles of human cognition and perception.

  • Brain Imaging Techniques: Modern cognitive science also utilizes advanced brain imaging techniques to study neural activity directly. Technologies such as Electroencephalography (EEG) and functional Magnetic Resonance Imaging (fMRI) are used to study brain activity and neural processes during cognitive tasks. EEG measures electrical activity in the brain using electrodes placed on the scalp, while fMRI detects changes in blood flow related to neural activity. These techniques provide increasingly detailed insights into the neural correlates of human thought, offering a more direct window into the biological basis of cognition.

The interdisciplinary field of Cognitive Science is specifically dedicated to this "thinking humanly" approach. Cognitive science integrates insights from psychology, computer science, philosophy, neuroscience, and linguistics to understand and model the human mind. However, Professor Mizero cautioned that just because an Artificial Intelligencesystem achieves human-level performance on a task, it does not automatically imply that it is thinking in a human-like way. He used the example of chess-playing programs, noting that while they can now defeat even the best human players, they employ very different strategies than human chess players. AI chess programs rely on brute-force computation and vast search spaces, whereas human players utilize intuition, pattern recognition, and strategic understanding in ways that are not directly replicated in current AI systems. Conversely, Professor Mizero also pointed out that simply mimicking human thought processes does not necessarily guarantee optimal performance. Human cognition is often subject to biases, limitations, and irrationalities. Therefore, directly replicating human thinking in AI systems might inadvertently reproduce these flaws, rather than achieving truly intelligent or optimal behavior.

Thinking Rationally: Logic and the Pursuit of Ideal Thought Processes

The third approach to defining Artificial Intelligenceshifts the focus from human-like thinking to rational thinking. This approach emphasizes the development of systems that think rationally, meaning they use logic and formal reasoning to arrive at correct or optimal conclusions. Instead of modeling human thought, this approach aims to embody ideal thought processes, based on principles of logic and rationality. It prioritizes the correctness and soundness of reasoning, seeking to create systems that reason flawlessly and avoid the errors and inconsistencies that can characterize human thinking.

Rational Thought: Rational thought is defined as reasoning that is logically sound and consistently leads to valid conclusions when starting from given premises. It often involves the use of formal systems of logic to represent knowledge and perform inferences. The core aim of rational thought is to ensure correctness and consistency in the reasoning process, adhering to the principles of logical validity and soundness.

Historically, logic played a central and dominant role in the field of Artificial Intelligence, particularly during what is often referred to as the "logicist tradition". In this tradition, logic was seen as the fundamental tool for building intelligent systems. Logic provides formal tools for representing knowledge in a precise and unambiguous manner, and for performing inferences based on that knowledge. A classic example of logical inference is Modus Ponens: if we know that "X is true" and "X implies Y is true," then logic allows us to validly infer that "Y is true." This type of deductive reasoning is a cornerstone of rational thought.

However, Professor Mizero highlighted a crucial caveat: while logic is a powerful tool for formalizing reasoning, human thinking is not solely based on logic. Although humans are capable of performing logical inferences, our cognitive processes are far more complex and encompass intuition, emotion, heuristics, and various forms of non-logical reasoning. To assume that human thought is purely logical is a simplification. Furthermore, pure logic may not be sufficient for dealing with uncertainty and real-world complexities. Real-world problems often involve incomplete information, probabilistic events, and ambiguous situations, which are not easily handled by classical logic alone.

The limitations of relying solely on logic in Artificial Intelligence became increasingly apparent in the 1990s. This realization led to a significant shift in the field, with a growing recognition of the need to incorporate methods for handling uncertainty. A pivotal figure in this shift was Judea Pearl, whose work in the 1980s and 1990s on probabilistic reasoning and Bayesian networks revolutionized the field. Pearl’s work demonstrated the power of probabilistic methods for representing and reasoning with uncertain information, providing a framework that could address many of the limitations of purely logic-based Artificial Intelligence. This integration of probabilistic reasoning, heavily influenced by Pearl’s contributions, marked a major turning point in the evolution of Artificial Intelligence, moving beyond the exclusive focus on logic to embrace more flexible and robust approaches for dealing with the complexities of real-world intelligence.

Acting Rationally: Rational Agents and the Pursuit of Optimal Behavior

The fourth and final approach to defining Artificial Intelligence, considered the "standard model" in Artificial Intelligence by Russell & Norvigand for the purposes of this course, focuses on the concept of rational agents. This approach emphasizes designing rational agents, which are entities that act in a way that is expected to achieve the best possible outcome or maximize their goals within a given environment. This definition shifts the focus from internal thought processes (whether human-like or ideal) to observable behavior and the effectiveness of actions in achieving desired outcomes.

Rational Agent: A rational agent is defined as an entity that perceives its environment, acts upon it, and consistently strives to achieve its goals in an optimal or best possible way. Rationality in this context is specifically defined as making choices and taking actions that are expected to maximize the agent’s goal achievement, given the agent’s current knowledge, beliefs, and the perceived state of the environment. The focus is on effective action and goal-oriented behavior, rather than on mimicking human-like thought or adhering to purely logical reasoning processes.

A rational agent is more than just a piece of software; it is conceived as an autonomous entity with several key characteristics:

  • Perceives its Environment: A rational agent must be able to perceive its environment through sensors or input mechanisms. This involves receiving sensory inputs from its surroundings, gathering information about the current state of the world it operates in. Perception is the foundation for an agent’s awareness and ability to make informed decisions.

  • Acts Autonomously: A defining feature of a rational agent is its capacity to act autonomously. This means it can independently take actions in its environment without constant external control or human intervention. Autonomy is crucial for agents to be proactive and responsive to changes in their environment.

  • Has Defined Goals: A rational agent operates with defined goals. It aims to achieve specific objectives, which may be pre-programmed, learned, or dynamically set. Goals provide direction and purpose to the agent’s actions, guiding its decision-making process.

  • Strives for Optimality: A rational agent is characterized by its drive to strive for optimality. It attempts to perform actions that are most likely to lead to goal achievement, often operating in environments characterized by uncertainty. This involves making decisions that maximize expected utility or minimize expected costs, given the available information and probabilistic nature of outcomes.

Rational agents may employ logical inference when appropriate for reasoning and decision-making, but they are not limited to purely logical approaches. They can also incorporate reflex-like actions for rapid responses in time-critical situations. For example, a rational agent designed to navigate a physical space might use logical reasoning for path planning but also incorporate reflexive responses to avoid immediate obstacles. Professor Mizero illustrated this with the example of a child touching a hot flame: a rational agent, like a child, would quickly withdraw its "hand" from a hot flame almost instantaneously, without engaging in complex, deliberative reasoning. This immediate, reflexive action is crucial for survival and efficient interaction with the environment.

Crucially, the concept of rationality in this context necessitates the definition of goals and a clear measure of success. A rational agent’s performance is evaluated based on its progress towards achieving its goals. This requires defining what constitutes goal achievement and establishing metrics to assess how effectively the agent is progressing or has progressed towards those goals. As Professor Mizero reiterated, this rational agent approach is considered the primary focus of modern Artificial Intelligence and is the perspective adopted by Russell & Norvigin their textbook. While acknowledging that other definitions and research directions exist within the broader field of Artificial Intelligence, this course will primarily adopt the rational agent perspective as a guiding framework. This approach emphasizes building systems that perform effectively and optimally in achieving their goals, rather than necessarily mimicking or replicating human intelligence in all its facets.

## A Framework for Understanding AI: Four Definitional Approaches

To further refine the definition of Artificial Intelligenceand provide a
structured framework for understanding its various facets, Professor
Mizero introduced a categorization scheme adapted from Russell & Norvig.
This framework organizes different approaches to defining Artificial
Intelligencealong two key dimensions, creating a 2x2 matrix of
definitional perspectives. These dimensions help to clarify the
underlying assumptions and goals that shape different conceptions of
what Artificial Intelligenceis and what it should strive to achieve.

The two dimensions structuring this framework are:

-   **Focus on Thought Processes (Thinking) vs. Observable Behavior
    (Acting):** This dimension distinguishes between definitions that
    prioritize the internal cognitive mechanisms of Artificial
    Intelligencesystems (*thinking*) and those that focus on the
    externally observable actions and performance of these systems
    (*acting*). Definitions emphasizing \"thinking\" are concerned with
    replicating or modeling the internal processes of intelligence,
    while definitions emphasizing \"acting\" are more concerned with
    achieving intelligent behavior, regardless of the underlying
    mechanism.

-   **Goal of Human-like Performance (Human-like) vs. Ideal or Optimal
    Performance (Rational):** This dimension differentiates between
    definitions that aim to create Artificial Intelligencesystems that
    perform tasks in a way that is similar to humans (*human-like*) and
    those that aim to achieve the best possible or most rational
    outcome, even if it deviates from typical human behavior (*rational*
    or *optimal*). The \"human-like\" goal emphasizes imitation of human
    intelligence, while the \"rational\" goal prioritizes effectiveness
    and optimality, potentially exceeding human limitations.

By crossing these two dimensions, Russell & Norvigand Professor Mizero
presented four distinct approaches to defining Artificial Intelligence,
each representing a different emphasis and set of objectives. These four
approaches are:

### Acting Humanly: The Turing Test and Behavioral Mimicry

This approach to defining Artificial Intelligencecenters on the goal of
constructing systems that exhibit behavior indistinguishable from that
of a human. The foundational concept for this approach is the **Turing
Test**, proposed by Alan Turing in his seminal 1950 paper.

::: tcolorbox
**Turing Test:** The *Turing Test* is defined as a test of a machine's
capacity to demonstrate intelligent behavior that is equivalent to, or
*indistinguishable from*, that of a human. In the original setup
envisioned by Turing, a human evaluator engages in natural language
conversations with two entities, both hidden from view. One entity is a
human, and the other is a machine. The evaluator's task is to determine,
solely based on the conversational exchanges, which entity is the
machine and which is the human. If the evaluator, after a sustained
conversation, cannot reliably differentiate the machine from the human,
then the machine is considered to have *passed the Turing Test*. Passing
this test is interpreted as evidence that the machine has achieved a
level of intelligence comparable to a human, at least in the context of
natural language interaction.
:::

For a machine to successfully pass the Turing Test, it must possess a
range of sophisticated capabilities, encompassing several key areas of
Artificial Intelligence:

-   **Natural Language Processing (NLP):** Essential for enabling the
    machine to *communicate effectively in human language*. This
    includes the ability to understand natural language input
    (questions, statements) and generate coherent and contextually
    appropriate natural language responses. NLP is crucial for the
    machine to engage in meaningful conversation and convincingly mimic
    human linguistic abilities.

-   **Knowledge Representation:** The machine needs robust *knowledge
    representation* mechanisms to *store and represent information about
    the world*. This knowledge base is necessary for the machine to draw
    upon relevant facts, concepts, and relationships to answer
    questions, provide context, and participate in conversations in an
    informed manner. Effective knowledge representation allows the
    machine to access and utilize information as humans do.

-   **Automated Reasoning:** To utilize the stored knowledge
    effectively, the machine must possess *automated reasoning*
    capabilities. This involves the ability to *use stored information
    to answer questions* logically, *draw conclusions* from given
    premises, and make inferences. Automated reasoning allows the
    machine to process information, solve problems, and generate new
    knowledge from existing knowledge, mimicking human cognitive
    processes.

-   **Machine Learning (ML):** Crucially, the machine should incorporate
    *machine learning* to *adapt to new information and improve over
    time*. Learning allows the machine to refine its responses, expand
    its knowledge base, and become more adept at conversation through
    experience. Machine learning is essential for creating systems that
    are not static but can evolve and enhance their performance,
    mirroring human learning and adaptation.

Turing also conceived of an extended version of the test, often referred
to as the **Total Turing Test**, which adds further requirements for
embodied intelligence and physical interaction:

-   **Computer Vision:** For a Total Turing Test, the machine would need
    *computer vision* capabilities to *perceive the world visually*.
    This would enable the machine to process and interpret visual
    information from its environment, similar to human visual
    perception. Vision is a crucial sensory modality for humans, and
    incorporating it into the Turing Test expands the scope of
    intelligent behavior being assessed.

-   **Robotics:** Furthermore, the Total Turing Test would necessitate
    *robotics* capabilities, allowing the machine to *physically
    interact with and manipulate objects in the world*. This embodiment
    would enable the machine to not only converse intelligently but also
    act intelligently within a physical environment, further blurring
    the lines between machine and human behavior. Robotics adds the
    dimension of physical agency and interaction to the assessment of
    machine intelligence.

Professor Mizero emphasized that while the Turing Test remains a highly
influential and conceptually significant benchmark in the field of
Artificial Intelligence, it also has inherent *limitations*. One key
limitation is that a system might potentially *pass the test through
clever simulation* and mimicry of human conversation patterns, without
possessing genuine \"intelligence\" or deep understanding. Such a system
might rely on sophisticated pattern matching and pre-programmed
responses rather than true cognitive processing. Furthermore, in domains
where Artificial Intelligencehas already surpassed human capabilities,
such as in *chess or medical diagnosis*, the goal of solely *mimicking
human behavior might be counterproductive*. In these scenarios, aiming
for human-level performance might actually mean intentionally limiting
the system's potential. For instance, to appear convincingly human in a
medical diagnosis Turing Test, an Artificial Intelligencesystem might
need to *intentionally make mistakes* at a rate comparable to human
doctors, which is clearly not the desired objective in real-world
medical applications. This highlights that while acting humanly is one
approach to defining Artificial Intelligence, it is not without its
drawbacks and may not always align with the ultimate goals of creating
truly intelligent and beneficial systems.

### Thinking Humanly: Cognitive Modeling and Understanding Human Thought Processes

This second approach to defining Artificial Intelligenceshifts the focus
from external behavior to internal cognitive processes. The goal here is
to build systems that not only act like humans but also *think like
humans*. This approach, known as *cognitive modeling*, centers on
developing computational models of human thought processes. It involves
a deep investigation into the workings of the human mind, aiming to
understand and ultimately *replicate the internal mechanisms of human
cognition*.

To achieve this, researchers in cognitive modeling employ various
methods to study and analyze human thought processes:

-   **Introspection and Self-Analysis:** One method involves
    *introspection*, where researchers analyze their *own thought
    processes*. This subjective approach involves careful
    self-observation and reflection on one's own cognitive experiences.
    While introspection can provide valuable insights, it is also
    inherently limited by its subjective nature and the potential for
    biases in self-reporting.

-   **Psychological Experiments and Behavioral Studies:** A more
    empirical approach involves conducting *psychological experiments*
    and *behavioral studies* to understand human cognitive functions.
    These experiments are designed to probe specific aspects of human
    cognition, such as perception, memory, language, and reasoning.
    Professor Mizero cited the example of the **Bouba-Kiki effect** as
    an illustration of psychological experimentation.

    ::: tcolorbox
    **Bouba-Kiki Effect:** The *Bouba-Kiki effect* is a psychological
    phenomenon demonstrating a non-arbitrary mapping between speech
    sounds and visual shapes. In this experiment, participants are
    typically shown two shapes: one rounded and curvilinear, and the
    other angular and spiky. They are then asked to associate each shape
    with one of two pseudowords: \"Bouba\" and \"Kiki.\" Across diverse
    cultures and languages, a strong majority of participants
    consistently associate the rounded shape with \"Bouba\" and the
    spiky shape with \"Kiki.\" This effect suggests that human sound
    symbolism and cross-modal sensory correspondence play a role in
    shaping our intuitive associations between language and visual
    forms. Experiments like this provide insights into the underlying
    principles of human cognition and perception.
    :::

    Such experiments, like the Bouba-Kiki effect study, provide
    empirical data that can inform the development of cognitive models
    and help researchers understand the underlying principles of human
    cognition and perception.

-   **Brain Imaging Techniques:** Modern cognitive science also utilizes
    advanced *brain imaging techniques* to study neural activity
    directly. Technologies such as *Electroencephalography (EEG)* and
    *functional Magnetic Resonance Imaging (fMRI)* are used to *study
    brain activity and neural processes* during cognitive tasks. EEG
    measures electrical activity in the brain using electrodes placed on
    the scalp, while fMRI detects changes in blood flow related to
    neural activity. These techniques provide increasingly detailed
    insights into the neural correlates of human thought, offering a
    more direct window into the biological basis of cognition.

The interdisciplinary field of **Cognitive Science** is specifically
dedicated to this \"thinking humanly\" approach. Cognitive science
integrates insights from psychology, computer science, philosophy,
neuroscience, and linguistics to *understand and model the human mind*.
However, Professor Mizero cautioned that just because an Artificial
Intelligencesystem *achieves human-level performance on a task*, it does
not automatically imply that it is *thinking in a human-like way*. He
used the example of *chess-playing programs*, noting that while they can
now defeat even the best human players, they employ very *different
strategies than human chess players*. AI chess programs rely on
brute-force computation and vast search spaces, whereas human players
utilize intuition, pattern recognition, and strategic understanding in
ways that are not directly replicated in current AI systems. Conversely,
Professor Mizero also pointed out that simply *mimicking human thought
processes* does not necessarily guarantee *optimal performance*. Human
cognition is often subject to biases, limitations, and irrationalities.
Therefore, directly replicating human thinking in AI systems might
inadvertently reproduce these flaws, rather than achieving truly
intelligent or optimal behavior.

### Thinking Rationally: Logic and the Pursuit of Ideal Thought Processes

The third approach to defining Artificial Intelligenceshifts the focus
from human-like thinking to *rational thinking*. This approach
emphasizes the development of systems that *think rationally*, meaning
they use logic and formal reasoning to arrive at correct or optimal
conclusions. Instead of modeling human thought, this approach aims to
embody *ideal thought processes*, based on principles of logic and
rationality. It prioritizes the correctness and soundness of reasoning,
seeking to create systems that reason flawlessly and avoid the errors
and inconsistencies that can characterize human thinking.

::: tcolorbox
**Rational Thought:** *Rational thought* is defined as reasoning that is
*logically sound* and consistently *leads to valid conclusions* when
starting from given premises. It often involves the use of *formal
systems of logic* to represent knowledge and perform inferences. The
core aim of rational thought is to ensure *correctness and consistency*
in the reasoning process, adhering to the principles of logical validity
and soundness.
:::

Historically, *logic* played a central and dominant role in the field of
Artificial Intelligence, particularly during what is often referred to
as the *\"logicist tradition\"*. In this tradition, logic was seen as
the fundamental tool for building intelligent systems. *Logic provides
formal tools for representing knowledge* in a precise and unambiguous
manner, and for performing *inferences* based on that knowledge. A
classic example of logical inference is *Modus Ponens*: if we know that
\"X is true\" and \"X implies Y is true,\" then logic allows us to
validly infer that \"Y is true.\" This type of deductive reasoning is a
cornerstone of rational thought.

However, Professor Mizero highlighted a crucial caveat: while logic is a
powerful tool for formalizing reasoning, *human thinking is not solely
based on logic*. Although humans are capable of performing logical
inferences, our cognitive processes are far more complex and encompass
intuition, emotion, heuristics, and various forms of non-logical
reasoning. To assume that human thought is purely logical is a
simplification. Furthermore, *pure logic may not be sufficient for
dealing with uncertainty and real-world complexities*. Real-world
problems often involve incomplete information, probabilistic events, and
ambiguous situations, which are not easily handled by classical logic
alone.

The *limitations of relying solely on logic in Artificial Intelligence*
became increasingly apparent in the 1990s. This realization led to a
significant shift in the field, with a growing recognition of the need
to incorporate methods for handling *uncertainty*. A pivotal figure in
this shift was *Judea Pearl*, whose work in the 1980s and 1990s on
probabilistic reasoning and Bayesian networks revolutionized the field.
Pearl's work demonstrated the power of *probabilistic methods* for
representing and reasoning with uncertain information, providing a
framework that could address many of the limitations of purely
logic-based Artificial Intelligence. This integration of probabilistic
reasoning, heavily influenced by Pearl's contributions, marked a major
turning point in the evolution of Artificial Intelligence, moving beyond
the exclusive focus on logic to embrace more flexible and robust
approaches for dealing with the complexities of real-world intelligence.

### Acting Rationally: Rational Agents and the Pursuit of Optimal Behavior

The fourth and final approach to defining Artificial Intelligence,
considered the *\"standard model\" in Artificial Intelligence* by
Russell & Norvigand for the purposes of this course, focuses on the
concept of *rational agents*. This approach emphasizes designing
**rational agents**, which are entities that act in a way that is
expected to achieve the best possible outcome or maximize their goals
within a given environment. This definition shifts the focus from
internal thought processes (whether human-like or ideal) to *observable
behavior* and the *effectiveness of actions* in achieving desired
outcomes.

::: tcolorbox
**Rational Agent:** A *rational agent* is defined as an entity that
*perceives its environment*, *acts upon it*, and consistently *strives
to achieve its goals* in an *optimal or best possible way*.
*Rationality* in this context is specifically defined as making choices
and taking actions that are expected to *maximize the agent's goal
achievement*, given the agent's current knowledge, beliefs, and the
perceived state of the environment. The focus is on effective action and
goal-oriented behavior, rather than on mimicking human-like thought or
adhering to purely logical reasoning processes.
:::

A *rational agent* is more than just a piece of software; it is
conceived as an autonomous entity with several key characteristics:

-   **Perceives its Environment:** A rational agent must be able to
    *perceive its environment* through sensors or input mechanisms. This
    involves receiving sensory inputs from its surroundings, gathering
    information about the current state of the world it operates in.
    Perception is the foundation for an agent's awareness and ability to
    make informed decisions.

-   **Acts Autonomously:** A defining feature of a rational agent is its
    capacity to *act autonomously*. This means it can independently
    *take actions in its environment* without constant external control
    or human intervention. Autonomy is crucial for agents to be
    proactive and responsive to changes in their environment.

-   **Has Defined Goals:** A rational agent operates with *defined
    goals*. It *aims to achieve specific objectives*, which may be
    pre-programmed, learned, or dynamically set. Goals provide direction
    and purpose to the agent's actions, guiding its decision-making
    process.

-   **Strives for Optimality:** A rational agent is characterized by its
    drive to *strive for optimality*. It *attempts to perform actions
    that are most likely to lead to goal achievement*, often operating
    in environments characterized by *uncertainty*. This involves making
    decisions that maximize expected utility or minimize expected costs,
    given the available information and probabilistic nature of
    outcomes.

Rational agents may employ *logical inference* when appropriate for
reasoning and decision-making, but they are not limited to purely
logical approaches. They can also incorporate *reflex-like actions* for
rapid responses in time-critical situations. For example, a rational
agent designed to navigate a physical space might use logical reasoning
for path planning but also incorporate reflexive responses to avoid
immediate obstacles. Professor Mizero illustrated this with the example
of a child touching a hot flame: a rational agent, like a child, would
quickly *withdraw its \"hand\" from a hot flame* almost instantaneously,
without engaging in complex, deliberative reasoning. This immediate,
reflexive action is crucial for survival and efficient interaction with
the environment.

Crucially, the concept of rationality in this context necessitates the
definition of *goals* and a clear *measure of success*. A rational
agent's performance is evaluated based on its progress towards achieving
its goals. This requires defining what constitutes goal achievement and
establishing metrics to assess how effectively the agent is progressing
or has progressed towards those goals. As Professor Mizero reiterated,
this *rational agent approach* is considered the *primary focus of
modern Artificial Intelligence* and is the perspective adopted by
Russell & Norvigin their textbook. While acknowledging that other
definitions and research directions exist within the broader field of
Artificial Intelligence, this course will primarily adopt the rational
agent perspective as a guiding framework. This approach emphasizes
building systems that perform effectively and optimally in achieving
their goals, rather than necessarily mimicking or replicating human
intelligence in all its facets.
